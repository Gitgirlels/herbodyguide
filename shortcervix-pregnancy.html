Short Cervix in Pregnancy: The Science of Cervical Length and Preterm Birth Prevention

A short cervix — usually defined as a cervical length under 25 mm on transvaginal ultrasound between 16 and 24 weeks of pregnancy — is one of the strongest known predictors of spontaneous preterm birth. It is also, in most women, a silent finding: it is typically discovered because the cervix was measured, not because anything felt wrong. A measurable risk marker that usually causes no symptoms, combined with several proposed treatments, has made the short cervix one of the most intensively studied topics in obstetrics — and one where the evidence is more mixed than many summaries suggest. Preterm birth is the leading cause of death in children under five years worldwide, with an estimated 10.6% of births (14.84 million) occurring preterm in 2014. This guide examines how the cervix changes during pregnancy, how cervical length is measured and what it can and cannot predict, who is at higher risk, and what the randomized trials and systematic reviews actually show for vaginal progesterone, cervical cerclage, and cervical pessaries — including the trials with negative or conflicting results — alongside Australian and American guidance. It is an educational overview, not individual medical advice: anyone with a short cervix finding should discuss her own situation with her maternity care provider.

What Is a Short Cervix? Definitions That Are Often Confused

Three Related Terms, Three Different Meanings

Short cervix: A sonographic measurement, not a diagnosis of a disease. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Practice Bulletin 234 generally defines a short cervix as a length under 25 mm measured between 16 and 24 weeks of gestation. Earlier ACOG guidance used under 20 mm as the threshold for recommending vaginal progesterone in women without a prior preterm birth; Practice Bulletin 234 moved that threshold to under 25 mm. Research trials have used different cutoffs, which matters when interpreting their results: 15 mm or less (Fonseca 2007), 10 to 20 mm (Hassan 2011), 25 mm or less (the short-cervix criterion in OPPTIMUM and the individual patient data meta-analyses), and 20 mm or less (TOPS).
Cervical insufficiency: A clinical diagnosis. ACOG Practice Bulletin 142 describes it as the inability of the cervix to retain a pregnancy in the second trimester in the absence of contractions or labor — classically painless cervical dilation. Estimates of how often it occurs vary widely, from roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 2000 pregnancies according to the MSD Manual. A short cervix on ultrasound raises the risk of preterm delivery but, per ACOG, is not on its own sufficient to diagnose cervical insufficiency.
Preterm labor: Contractions accompanied by cervical change before 37 weeks. This is a symptomatic condition managed with different protocols. The progesterone trials discussed below (Fonseca 2007; Hassan 2011) enrolled asymptomatic women, so their results do not directly apply to women who are already in threatened preterm labor.

A short cervix is a risk marker, not a prediction that preterm birth will happen. In the foundational NICHD Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units cohort of 2915 women (Iams et al., 1996), spontaneous delivery before 35 weeks occurred in 4.3% of women overall. ACOG Practice Bulletin 234, as summarised in American Family Physician, reports a positive predictive value of 33% for a cervical length under 25 mm before 24 weeks and a negative predictive value of 92% for a length above 25 mm. In plain terms: most women with a short cervix still do not deliver early without treatment, and a normal measurement is more reassuring than a short one is alarming.
Analogy: Think of three different things a smoke detector setup can tell you. A short cervix is like a reading from a sensor: it is a measurement, taken at one moment. Cervical insufficiency is like a confirmed diagnosis that the building's wiring keeps failing, a conclusion drawn from the whole picture and history. Preterm labor is the alarm actually going off. A reading can be a warning sign without the alarm sounding, which is why the three terms should not be used interchangeably. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)
Analogy: The predictive values quoted above work like a weather forecast. A forecast of rain (a short cervix, about 33% positive predictive value) means an umbrella is sensible, but most such days still stay dry. A forecast of clear skies (a normal cervix, about 92% negative predictive value) is reassuring, though not a guarantee. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Why Cervical Length Matters: Preterm Birth in Numbers

Chawanpaiboon et al. (2019, Lancet Global Health) estimated that 10.6% of live births worldwide were preterm in 2014 (uncertainty interval 9.0–12.0%), equating to 14.84 million babies (12.65–16.73 million). Of these, 12.0 million (81.1%) occurred in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa. Regional rates ranged from 13.4% in North Africa to 8.7% in Europe, and among the 38 countries with high-quality data, rates increased between 2000 and 2014 in 26 and decreased in 12. In the United States, the National Center for Health Statistics reports a preterm birth rate of 10.41% in 2023 (373,902 preterm births), essentially unchanged from 10.38% in 2022 (Martin et al., NCHS Data Brief 507).

Spontaneous Versus Medically Indicated Preterm Birth

A short cervix relates to spontaneous preterm birth, which is only part of the overall preterm birth rate. In Victoria, Australia, singleton preterm birth rose from 5.9% to 6.4% between 2007 and 2017, a rise attributed to an increase in iatrogenic (medically indicated) preterm birth from 2.5% to 3.6% (Burger et al., 2021, Acta Obstetricia et Gynecologica Scandinavica). This distinction is important for realistic expectations: measuring and treating the cervix targets spontaneous early birth, and cannot be expected to change births that are brought forward deliberately because of maternal or fetal complications.

How the Evidence Developed: A Timeline

Key Milestones in Cervical Length Research

1990: Andersen and colleagues publish an early study showing that ultrasonographic cervical length measurement can help predict preterm delivery risk
1995: Iams and colleagues describe "cervical competence as a continuum," showing that cervical length varies along a spectrum rather than being simply normal or incompetent
1996: The NICHD Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units Network publishes the landmark prospective cohort (2915 women examined at about 24 weeks, 2531 again at about 28 weeks) showing that the risk of spontaneous preterm delivery rises steadily as cervical length falls
2001: Owen and colleagues show, in women at high risk, that mid-trimester transvaginal measurement predicts spontaneous preterm birth before 35 weeks
2005: Berghella's individual patient data meta-analysis finds that cerclage does not prevent preterm birth in all women with a short cervix, but may help singleton pregnancies with a prior preterm birth — and appears to increase risk in twins
2007: Fonseca et al. (NEJM) report that vaginal progesterone reduces early preterm delivery in women found at routine screening to have a cervix of 15 mm or less
2009: Owen's multicenter randomized trial of cerclage in women with a prior spontaneous preterm birth and a cervix under 25 mm
2011: Hassan et al. publish the PREGNANT trial of vaginal progesterone gel for a cervix of 10 to 20 mm
2012: The PECEP trial (Goya et al.) reports a large benefit from a cervical pessary; Romero's first individual patient data meta-analysis supports vaginal progesterone
2016: Two major trials challenge the earlier enthusiasm: Nicolaides et al. find no benefit from a pessary, and the OPPTIMUM trial (Norman et al.) finds no significant benefit from vaginal progesterone in its mixed high-risk population
2017: Western Australia reports the first statewide multifaceted preterm birth prevention program, including universal cervical length measurement; the Cochrane review of cerclage and the Berghella meta-analysis of cerclage without prior preterm birth are published
2018: Romero's updated meta-analysis including OPPTIMUM data still finds benefit of vaginal progesterone in singleton pregnancies with a short cervix; the Australian Preterm Birth Prevention Alliance is formed
2021: ACOG Practice Bulletin 234 sets the vaginal progesterone threshold at under 25 mm
2022: The ISUOG practice guideline on ultrasound prediction of spontaneous preterm birth is published
2023: The TOPS trial (Hoffman et al.) finds no benefit from a pessary and a higher rate of fetal or neonatal death in the pessary group; an individual patient data meta-analysis finds no effect of cerclage for a short cervix detected after 24 weeks

Anatomy and Physiology: How the Cervix Changes in Pregnancy

The Cervix as a Barrier

The cervix has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. During pregnancy it must stay firmly closed, acting as a structural barrier that holds the pregnancy in and as a barrier against microorganisms ascending from the vagina. At birth it must soften, shorten, and open to a degree unmatched by any other tissue. Word et al. (2007, Seminars in Reproductive Medicine) describe the connective tissue remodeling of the cervix around parturition as unparalleled in scope and magnitude among biological processes.

The Four Phases of Cervical Remodeling

Softening: A slow, progressive phase that begins early in pregnancy, well before any shortening can be measured. Changes in collagen structure — reduced cross-linking and increased collagen solubility — make the tissue more compliant while it remains strong. In mouse studies (Read et al., 2007, Reproduction), softening begins between gestation days 10 and 12, and the softened cervix still maintains its tissue strength.
Ripening: A later phase in which the connective tissue becomes more disorganized and loses strength, preparing the cervix for dilation. In serial cervical length studies in women, Word et al. report that cervical ripening precedes the contractions of labor by several weeks.
Dilation: The opening of the cervix during labor.
Postpartum repair: Return of the cervix toward its pre-pregnancy structure after birth, a stage in which immune cells appear to play a regulatory role (Timmons et al., 2010, Trends in Endocrinology and Metabolism).
The phases overlap in time, and each is regulated by a different set of mechanisms (Word et al., 2007).

This sequence helps explain why a short cervix can appear before a woman feels anything. If ripening and shortening begin weeks before the contractions of labor, then a cervix that has shortened prematurely can be detected by ultrasound at a time when the pregnancy feels entirely normal. Much of the detailed molecular work on remodeling comes from rodent models, though Timmons et al. (2010) note evidence that key mechanisms are well conserved between rodents and humans — so the human biology is better established in outline than in molecular detail.
Analogy: Picture the cervix as the drawstring of a bag holding something precious. For most of the pregnancy the drawstring must stay tight. In the softening phase the cord is slowly becoming more flexible while still holding firm, like a new rope being worked in. In ripening it loosens further, like fruit softening before it is ready to pick. Dilation is the bag finally opening, and repair is the cord tightening back up afterward. A short cervix can be thought of as the cord starting to loosen earlier than planned. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Why Does a Cervix Shorten Early?

The cause of cervical shortening is not fully understood, and it likely differs between women.

Proposed Contributors

Structural and genetic factors: The MSD Manual describes the cause of cervical insufficiency as not well understood, seeming to involve a combination of structural abnormalities and biochemical factors such as inflammation or infection, which may be acquired or genetic. Congenital collagen disorders such as Ehlers-Danlos syndrome are recognized risk factors.
Previous cervical trauma: Prior cone biopsy (particularly when a larger amount of tissue was removed), trachelectomy, and prior deep cervical lacerations from vaginal or cesarean delivery are described as risk factors (MSD Manual; ACOG Practice Bulletin 142).
Uterine and developmental factors: Müllerian duct anomalies (such as a bicornuate or septate uterus) and in utero diethylstilbestrol (DES) exposure are listed as risk factors in ACOG Practice Bulletin 142.
Reduced progesterone action: The rationale for progesterone treatment rests on the hypothesis that a decline in progesterone action contributes to a short cervix and subsequent preterm labor (Romero et al., 2024, Clinical Obstetrics and Gynecology). This is a hypothesis supporting a treatment, and should not be read as proof that women with a short cervix have low progesterone levels.

Who Is at Higher Risk?

Established Risk Factors

Several factors increase the likelihood of a short cervix or of preterm birth in women who have one. These are the basis for deciding which women should have serial cervical length measurements rather than a single scan.

Risk Factors Listed in Australian and American Sources

Previous spontaneous preterm birth: Listed as a risk factor in Australian guidance (Withanawasam and Tara, 2019, Australian Journal of General Practice). ACOG recommends serial transvaginal cervical length screening between 16 weeks 0 days and 24 weeks 0 days in singleton pregnancies with a previous spontaneous preterm birth.
Previous cervical surgery: Cone biopsy and large loop excision of the transformation zone (LLETZ/LEEP) are listed as risk factors (Withanawasam and Tara, 2019).
Previous second-trimester loss: Particularly painless losses, which raise the suspicion of cervical insufficiency (ACOG Practice Bulletin 142; MSD Manual).
Uterine anomalies and in utero DES exposure: See above.
Multiple pregnancy: Twin pregnancies have a different risk profile and a different evidence base for treatment, discussed separately below.

Cervical Surgery: What the Largest Meta-Analysis Shows

Treatment of cervical precancer (CIN) by cone biopsy or loop excision is common in women of reproductive age, and its relationship to later preterm birth has been studied extensively. The most comprehensive analysis is the systematic review and meta-analysis by Kyrgiou et al. (2016, BMJ), which included 71 studies, about 65,000 treated women, and more than six million untreated women.

Kyrgiou et al. (2016, BMJ) found that local cervical treatment significantly increased the risk of overall preterm birth (before 37 weeks) from 5.4% to 10.7% (relative risk 1.78; 95% CI 1.60–1.98), severe preterm birth (before 32–34 weeks) from 1.4% to 3.5% (RR 2.40; 1.92–2.99), and extreme preterm birth (before 28–30 weeks) from 0.3% to 1.0% (RR 2.54; 1.77–3.63). Risk increased with cone depth: relative risks were 1.54 (1.09–2.18) for depths of 10–12 mm or less, 1.93 (1.62–2.31) for 10–12 mm or more, 2.77 (1.95–3.93) for 15–17 mm or more, and 4.91 (2.06–11.68) for 20 mm or more. Risk was higher after more than one treatment (13.2% versus 4.1%; RR 3.78; 2.65–5.39), and higher after excision than after ablation. Importantly, women with CIN who had not yet been treated also had a higher baseline rate of preterm birth than the general population (5.9% versus 5.6%; RR 1.24; 1.14–1.35).
Two cautions apply. First, an accompanying BMJ editorial noted that the meta-analysis consisted mainly of retrospective cohort studies with the biases that design carries. Second, the finding that untreated women with CIN also have a higher background risk suggests that some of the excess risk after treatment reflects shared underlying factors (such as HPV infection) rather than the surgery alone — although risk remained elevated after treatment even when compared with women who had CIN but no treatment. Absolute risks are lower for shallower excisions, and most women who have had a loop excision go on to deliver at term.

Many Women With a Short Cervix Have No Risk Factors

The MSD Manual notes that most women with cervical insufficiency do not have known risk factors. This is why some guidelines favor measuring the cervix at the routine mid-pregnancy scan in all women, rather than only in those with a history — a point discussed under screening below.

Symptoms: Why a Short Cervix Is Usually Silent

What a Woman Can and Cannot Feel

The trials that established treatments for short cervix enrolled women who were asymptomatic — that is, without signs or symptoms of preterm labor (Fonseca 2007; Hassan 2011). The MSD Manual states that cervical insufficiency is often asymptomatic until preterm delivery occurs. Some women have earlier symptoms, which the MSD Manual lists as vaginal pressure, vaginal bleeding or spotting, nonspecific abdominal or lower back pain, and vaginal discharge. On examination the cervix may be soft, effaced, or dilated.

These symptoms are nonspecific — each is also common in uncomplicated pregnancy — so they cannot be used to screen for a short cervix. The finding is made by measurement, which is why the practice of measuring cervical length at the mid-pregnancy scan exists.

Analogy: A short cervix is a little like early high blood pressure or slowly rising cholesterol: it is found by measuring, not by feeling. Because you cannot sense it, the measurement itself is the only way to find it, which is why it is checked at the mid-pregnancy scan. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

When to Contact a Maternity Provider Promptly

Contact your maternity unit, midwife, or doctor promptly — or attend an emergency department — if you have:

Regular tightening or painful contractions before 37 weeks
Increasing pelvic or vaginal pressure
Vaginal bleeding or spotting
A gush or ongoing leak of fluid
A change in vaginal discharge
New or worsening lower back or lower abdominal pain

These are general warning signs of possible preterm labor or cervical change. The MSD Manual specifically lists vaginal pressure, bleeding or spotting, abdominal or lower back pain, and discharge as symptoms that can accompany cervical insufficiency. They do not mean preterm birth is certain, and many have benign causes, but they warrant clinical assessment rather than waiting. Women who already have a cerclage or pessary in place should follow the specific instructions they were given about when to call.

Measuring the Cervix

The Standard Technique

Transvaginal ultrasound is the accepted method for measuring cervical length. Measurement technique matters because small errors change which side of a 25 mm threshold a woman falls on. The ISUOG 2022 practice guideline sets out the following approach.

ISUOG Technique for Transvaginal Cervical Length Measurement

Preparation: The maternal bladder should be empty, with the woman semi-recumbent and legs abducted. A high-frequency transvaginal transducer is covered with a disposable sheath and gel.
Landmarks: The inferior border of the bladder, the external os, the endocervical canal and mucosa, and the internal os (defined by the edge of the endocervical mucosa) must all be visible.
Pressure and magnification: Minimize probe pressure so that both cervical lips have the same width, and magnify the image so the cervix occupies 50 to 75% of the screen.
Measurement: Place calipers at the functional internal os and external os in a straight line. Obtain three distinct measurements and record the shortest technically correct one, allowing enough time to observe dynamic changes.
Pitfalls: A full bladder or excessive transducer pressure can artificially lengthen the cervix. A thickened lower uterine segment or uterine contraction can mimic funneling, so the cervical mucosa must be identified properly.

Analogy: Measuring a cervix is like measuring a soft sponge with a tape measure. Press too hard and the sponge compresses or changes shape, so the reading is misleading. That is why technique rules call for light probe pressure, an empty bladder, and recording the shortest of several good measurements, so one lucky reading does not hide a real finding. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Transabdominal Versus Transvaginal Measurement

The mid-pregnancy morphology (anatomy) scan is a routine part of antenatal care in Australia and the United States. Australian guidance (Withanawasam and Tara, 2019) states that the Australasian Society for Ultrasound in Medicine recommends including cervical length in all mid-trimester morphology scans. On a transabdominal scan, a cervical length above 35 mm is sufficient to exclude a short cervix; if it is under 35 mm, or the cervix cannot be adequately seen, a transvaginal scan should be performed. The transvaginal approach is described as the gold standard by NICE, SOGC, SMFM, and ISUOG.

Funneling — protrusion of the fetal membranes into the upper cervical canal — is often reported alongside cervical length, but cervical length is the measurement that has emerged as the most reliable predictor. Berghella (2004, Contemporary OB/GYN) notes that funneling is difficult to measure consistently and varies between examiners, and that the internal os is open in about 25 to 33% of high-risk women. The cervix can also change during a single examination: it may shorten in response to fundal pressure in about 5% of cases, which is why the shortest technically correct measurement is the one recorded.

Who Should Be Screened?

There is broad agreement that women at high risk should have cervical length measured, and genuine disagreement about universal screening.

Where Guidelines Agree and Where They Differ

High-risk women (agreement): A systematic review of 49 clinical practice guidelines from 16 developers (Medley et al., 2018, BJOG) found consensus for cervical length screening in high-risk women. ACOG recommends serial transvaginal measurements from 16 to 24 weeks in singleton pregnancies with a prior spontaneous preterm birth. Serial surveillance every one to two weeks has been suggested in this group (Vidaeff, 2009, OBG Management).
Universal screening (contested): A 2024 systematic review of preterm birth guidelines using the AGREE II framework found consensus on cervical length screening in high-risk pregnancies, but identified universal cervical length measurement, preterm birth clinics, biomarkers, and cervical pessaries as areas of contention or limited consensus.
Australian recommendations: The Australian Preterm Birth Prevention Alliance (2021 synopsis) recommends including cervical length measurement in all mid-pregnancy morphology scans performed between 18 and 20 weeks. A survey of Western Australian obstetric care providers (Pedretti et al., 2022) found that practice remained focused on women with obvious risk factors rather than universal screening, and that the definition of a short cervix and its management remained varied among providers.

What a Short Cervix Does and Does Not Predict

Risk Rises Continuously as Length Falls

The 1996 NICHD study showed that cervical length is normally distributed in pregnancy and that risk increases continuously rather than at a single cutoff. At about 24 weeks the mean cervical length was 35.2 mm (standard deviation 8.3 mm); at about 28 weeks it was 33.7 mm (SD 8.5 mm). The 25 mm cutoff used in clinical practice sits close to the 10th percentile (26 mm in that cohort).

Relative Risk of Spontaneous Delivery Before 35 Weeks (Iams et al., 1996)

Compared with women whose cervical length at about 24 weeks was above the 75th percentile:
At or below the 75th percentile (40 mm): relative risk 1.98
At or below the 50th percentile (35 mm): relative risk 2.35
At or below the 25th percentile (30 mm): relative risk 3.79
At or below the 10th percentile (26 mm): relative risk 6.19
At or below the 5th percentile: relative risk 9.49
At or below the 1st percentile (13 mm): relative risk 13.99
For measurements at about 28 weeks the corresponding relative risks were higher, reaching 24.94 at or below the 1st percentile.

Analogy: A relative risk is like a lottery ticket sale: if a ticket's chance of winning doubles, that is a big relative change, but the absolute chance can still be small. This is why a relative risk of 6.19 for a very short cervix does not mean a 6-in-10 chance of early birth, and the percentages quoted above are the better guide to individual likelihood. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)
Relative risk is not the same as likelihood. In a cohort of women at high risk of spontaneous preterm birth, Owen et al. (2001, JAMA) found that a cervical length under 25 mm at the initial examination was associated with a relative risk of 3.3 (95% CI 2.1–5.0) for spontaneous preterm birth before 35 weeks, with a sensitivity of 19% and specificity of 98%. In other words, the finding was uncommon at that first scan, so most women who went on to deliver early did not have a short cervix at that first scan — which is why serial measurement was proposed for high-risk women and why a single normal scan cannot guarantee a term birth.
Analogy: The sensitivity of 19% is like a fishing net with very large holes. Most fish slip through (many early births occur in women whose first scan showed a normal cervix), yet the fish it does catch are real, and the net rarely catches anything that should be thrown back (specificity of 98%). A single normal scan is therefore reassuring but not a guarantee, which is why high-risk women may be scanned repeatedly. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Treatment Evidence: What the Trials Show

Vaginal Progesterone

Vaginal progesterone is the intervention with the strongest evidence in women with a short cervix and no prior preterm birth, and is the first-line option in ACOG and Australian guidance. Its evidence base, however, includes both strongly positive and neutral trials, and it is worth reading carefully.

The Two Landmark Placebo-Controlled Trials

Fonseca et al. (2007, NEJM): Cervical length was measured by transvaginal ultrasound at a median of 22 weeks (range 20–25) in 24,620 women attending routine prenatal care. A cervical length of 15 mm or less was found in 413 women (1.7%); 250 of these were randomized to vaginal progesterone 200 mg nightly or placebo from 24 to 34 weeks. The trial included both singleton and twin pregnancies. Spontaneous delivery before 34 weeks occurred in 19.2% of the progesterone group versus 34.4% of the placebo group (relative risk 0.56; 95% CI 0.36–0.86). The reduction in neonatal morbidity (8.1% versus 13.8%; RR 0.59; 0.26–1.25; P = 0.17) was not statistically significant, and there were no serious adverse events attributed to progesterone.
Hassan et al. (2011, Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology; the PREGNANT trial): 465 women with a singleton pregnancy and a cervical length of 10 to 20 mm at 19+0 to 23+6 weeks were randomized (458 analyzed: 235 progesterone, 223 placebo) to 90 mg of 8% vaginal progesterone gel daily from 20 to 23+6 weeks until 36+6 weeks, rupture of membranes, or delivery. Preterm birth before 33 weeks occurred in 8.9% versus 16.1% (RR 0.55; 0.33–0.92; P = 0.02). Progesterone also reduced birth before 28 weeks (5.1% versus 10.3%; RR 0.50; 0.25–0.97) and before 35 weeks (14.5% versus 23.3%; RR 0.62; 0.42–0.92), respiratory distress syndrome (3.0% versus 7.6%; RR 0.39; 0.17–0.92), any neonatal morbidity or mortality (7.7% versus 13.5%; RR 0.57; 0.33–0.99), and birthweight under 1500 g (6.4% versus 13.6%). The authors calculated that 14 women with a short cervix would need to be treated to prevent one preterm birth before 33 weeks.

Pooled Analyses

Romero et al. (2012, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology) pooled individual patient data from five trials (775 women and 827 infants) with a cervical length of 25 mm or less in the mid-trimester. Vaginal progesterone reduced preterm birth before 33 weeks (RR 0.58; 95% CI 0.42–0.80), before 35 weeks (RR 0.69; 0.55–0.88), and before 28 weeks (RR 0.50; 0.30–0.81), as well as respiratory distress syndrome (RR 0.48; 0.30–0.76), composite neonatal morbidity and mortality (RR 0.57; 0.40–0.81), birthweight under 1500 g (RR 0.55; 0.38–0.80), neonatal intensive care admission (RR 0.75), and mechanical ventilation (RR 0.66). There were no significant differences in adverse maternal events or congenital anomalies between progesterone and placebo groups.

The OPPTIMUM Trial: A Neutral Result

The OPPTIMUM trial (Norman et al., 2016, Lancet) randomized 1228 women at high risk of preterm birth (610 placebo, 618 progesterone) to 200 mg of vaginal progesterone daily from 22–24 weeks to 34 weeks. Eligibility was broader than the short-cervix trials: women qualified through a prior spontaneous preterm birth at 34 weeks or earlier, a cervical length of 25 mm or less, or a positive fetal fibronectin test combined with other risk factors. After adjustment for multiple comparisons, progesterone had no significant effect on the primary obstetric outcome (fetal death or birth before 34 weeks; 16% with progesterone versus 18% with placebo; adjusted odds ratio 0.86; 95% CI 0.61–1.22) or the neonatal composite outcome (OR 0.62; 0.38–1.03), and no effect on childhood cognitive score at two years (97.3 versus 97.7). Serious adverse events were similar (progesterone 10%, placebo 11%). The authors concluded that vaginal progesterone was not associated with reduced preterm birth or composite adverse neonatal outcomes in this population, with no long-term benefit or harm at age two.

Reconciling the Evidence

OPPTIMUM's broader eligibility makes its result hard to compare directly with trials restricted to a short cervix. When Romero et al. (2018, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology) pooled individual patient data for singleton pregnancies with a mid-trimester cervical length of 25 mm or less — an analysis undertaken after OPPTIMUM's publication had raised questions about the efficacy of vaginal progesterone — vaginal progesterone significantly decreased preterm birth before 36, 35, 34, 32, 30, and 28 weeks, as well as spontaneous preterm birth before 33 and 34 weeks, respiratory distress syndrome, and composite neonatal morbidity and mortality, without demonstrable deleterious effects on childhood neurodevelopment at two years. ACOG Practice Bulletin 234 recommends vaginal progesterone for asymptomatic singleton pregnancies with a short cervix (under 25 mm) and no prior preterm birth, and, as summarised in American Family Physician, quotes a number needed to treat of 14 to prevent one preterm birth before 33 weeks.

A note on disclosures: Several investigators in the early progesterone trials and meta-analyses disclosed relationships with manufacturers of vaginal progesterone products. Readers can reasonably weigh independent trials alongside industry-linked ones, and should check the disclosure statements in the original papers.

Dose, Formulation, and Timing

What Is and Is Not Established

The optimal dose and formulation have not been established. The trials used 200 mg nightly vaginal capsules (Fonseca; OPPTIMUM) and 90 mg of 8% gel (Hassan). Australian guidance (Withanawasam and Tara, 2019) states that doses between 90 and 200 mg daily have been used and that current Australian practice is to commence 200 mg of vaginal progesterone nightly and continue until 37 weeks; a New South Wales procedure notes that some guidelines recommend 400 mg (SESLHD, 2020). The same procedure notes that most progesterone trials for a short cervix excluded women with a cervical length under 10 mm, so evidence for very short cervices is thinner.
Intramuscular progesterone: ACOG Practice Bulletin 234, as summarised in American Family Physician, states that intramuscular progesterone does not appear to reduce preterm birth risk in singleton pregnancies with a short cervix and may increase risk in multiple pregnancies.

Analogy: A number needed to treat of 14 is like 14 people carrying umbrellas to keep one person dry. Thirteen of them would have stayed dry anyway or would have got wet regardless, but nobody can tell in advance who the fourteenth is, so everyone with a short cervix is offered treatment. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Cervical Cerclage

Cerclage is a surgical procedure in which a stitch is placed around the cervix to provide mechanical support. Whether it helps depends strongly on who receives it.

Women With a Prior Spontaneous Preterm Birth and a Short Cervix

Owen et al. (2009, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology): Of 1014 women with a prior spontaneous preterm birth before 34 weeks who were screened, 302 with a cervical length under 25 mm were randomized to cerclage or no cerclage. Delivery before 35 weeks occurred in 32% of the cerclage group and 42% of controls, a difference that did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.09). Previable birth (before 24 weeks; 6.1% versus 14%; P = 0.03) and perinatal death (8.8% versus 16%; P = 0.046) were less frequent with cerclage. In a stratified analysis, birth before 35 weeks was reduced in the under-15 mm stratum (P = 0.006), with no effect in the 15–24 mm stratum — a subgroup finding that Vidaeff (2009) noted came from a secondary analysis and that the 15 mm cutoff was somewhat arbitrary.
Berghella et al. (2011, Obstetrics and Gynecology): A meta-analysis of five trials (504 women with a singleton pregnancy, previous spontaneous preterm birth, and cervical length under 25 mm before 24 weeks) found that cerclage reduced preterm birth before 35 weeks from 41.3% to 28.4% (RR 0.70; 95% CI 0.55–0.89) and composite perinatal mortality and morbidity from 24.8% to 15.6% (RR 0.64).

Women With a Short Cervix and No Prior Preterm Birth

Berghella et al. (2017, Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology) pooled individual patient data from five randomized trials (419 asymptomatic singleton pregnancies with a short cervix under 25 mm and no prior spontaneous preterm birth) and concluded that cerclage does not appear to prevent preterm delivery or improve neonatal outcome, although it might be effective at shorter lengths and with adjunctive tocolytics or antibiotics — a finding the authors graded as low-quality evidence requiring confirmation. ACOG Practice Bulletin 234 describes ultrasound-indicated cerclage in this group as of uncertain benefit, with possible effectiveness only if the cervix is under 10 mm; Australian guidance (Withanawasam and Tara, 2019) states that cerclage is not generally recommended for an incidental short cervix, with individual consideration if the initial length is under 10 mm. A retrospective cohort of low-risk women with a cervical length of 25 mm or less (Wood et al., 2019, American Journal of Perinatology) found that cerclage did not decrease preterm birth compared with vaginal progesterone.
Short cervix found after 24 weeks: An individual patient data meta-analysis (Gulersen et al., 2023, AJOG MFM) of singleton pregnancies with a cervical length of 25 mm or less between 24 and 29+6 weeks found that cerclage neither reduced nor increased preterm birth.

Berghella et al. (2005, Obstetrics and Gynecology) pooled four trials and found that, in the total population, preterm birth before 35 weeks occurred in 29.2% (89 of 305) of the cerclage group versus 34.8% (105 of 302) of controls (RR 0.84; 95% CI 0.67–1.06). In singleton pregnancies the relative risk was 0.74 (0.57–0.96), in singletons with prior preterm birth 0.61 (0.40–0.92), and in singletons with prior second-trimester loss 0.57 (0.33–0.99). In twin pregnancies, however, cerclage was associated with significantly more preterm birth (RR 2.15; 1.15–4.01). The Cochrane review (Alfirevic et al., 2017) concluded that cerclage reduces preterm birth in women at high risk and probably reduces perinatal death, found no evidence that the effect differed between history-based and ultrasound-based indications (though data were limited), and noted that whether cerclage is better or worse than vaginal progesterone remains unanswered.

Exam-Indicated and History-Indicated Cerclage

Different cerclage indications exist beyond a short cervix on ultrasound. After uterine activity and intra-amniotic infection have been excluded, a physical examination–indicated ("rescue") cerclage may be considered when painless dilation of the cervix is found in the second trimester before 24 weeks, as described in ACOG Practice Bulletin 142. History-indicated cerclage — placed on the basis of a history strongly suggesting cervical insufficiency, typically recurrent second-trimester losses — is a separate decision from the short-cervix question and is described in the MSD Manual and ACOG Practice Bulletin 142.

Cervical Pessary

A cervical pessary is a flexible silicone ring placed around the cervix, intended to support it and change its angle. Pessary trials have produced strikingly inconsistent results, and the newest and largest evidence points away from benefit.

The Pessary Trials in Singleton Pregnancy

PECEP (Goya et al., 2012, Lancet): An open-label trial in five Spanish hospitals randomizing 385 women with a short cervix found at the routine 20–23 week scan to pessary or expectant management. Spontaneous delivery before 34 weeks was far less frequent with a pessary (12 of 190 [6%] versus 51 of 190 [27%]; odds ratio 0.18; 95% CI 0.08–0.37). The trial was not masked, which the authors acknowledged.
Nicolaides et al. (2016, NEJM): A multicenter randomized trial of 465 pessary and 467 control participants with a singleton pregnancy and a cervical length of 25 mm or less at 20+0 to 24+6 weeks. Participants in either group whose cervical length was 15 mm or less received vaginal progesterone. Spontaneous delivery before 34 weeks occurred in 12.0% with a pessary and 10.8% with expectant management (odds ratio 1.12; 0.75–1.69; P = 0.57), with no benefit.
Hui et al. (2013, American Journal of Perinatology): A smaller randomized trial from Hong Kong, which found no significant effect of a cerclage pessary on preterm birth before 34 or 37 weeks.
Meta-analysis: Jin et al. (2017, Scientific Reports) pooled three trials (1412 women) and found that a pessary did not reduce spontaneous preterm birth before 34 weeks (risk ratio 0.71; 95% CI 0.21–2.43), with the very wide interval reflecting the conflicting trial results.

The TOPS trial (Hoffman et al., 2023, JAMA), run by the NICHD Maternal-Fetal Medicine Units Network at 12 US centers, is the most recent large trial. It enrolled 544 women (64% of a planned 850) with a singleton pregnancy, a cervical length of 20 mm or less at 16+0 to 23+6 weeks, and no prior spontaneous preterm birth, randomizing 280 to a pessary and 264 to usual care. Delivery or fetal death before 37 weeks occurred in 45.5% with a pessary versus 45.6% with usual care (relative risk 1.00). Fetal or neonatal/infant death occurred in 13.3% of the pessary group versus 6.8% of the usual care group (relative risk 1.94; 95% CI 1.13–3.32). Recruitment was stopped early for futility and concern about mortality. The authors noted that the finding had not been reported in other randomized trials, and the NICHD noted that the usual care group was more likely to receive cerclage, which could have influenced results. Nearly all participants (98.9%) received vaginal progesterone. ACOG states that pessaries have not been proven effective in singleton pregnancy with a short cervix and no prior preterm birth.
Analogy: A cerclage works like a purse-string: a stitch placed around the opening to hold it closed mechanically. A pessary is more like a supportive collar or ring that sits around the cervix to hold its position and angle. Both are mechanical supports, whereas progesterone is a medicine meant to influence how the tissue behaves. The trials show that a good idea mechanically does not always work in practice, as the mixed pessary results show. (An analogy to aid understanding, not a finding from the studies cited.)

Choosing Between Progesterone, Cerclage, and Pessary

What Is Unresolved

No head-to-head answer: The Cochrane review (Alfirevic et al., 2017) found too few trials to determine whether cerclage is better or worse than vaginal progesterone.
Combining treatments: TOPS participants almost all received progesterone, so TOPS tested a pessary added to progesterone rather than instead of it. For women with a prior spontaneous preterm birth who are already receiving progesterone and then develop a cervical length under 25 mm, ACOG guidance is that cerclage should be considered.
Bed rest: The South Eastern Sydney Local Health District procedure on preterm birth prevention (2020) states that bed rest is not recommended to prevent preterm birth in women at increased risk through a previous preterm birth or a short cervix.

Twin Pregnancy

The evidence for twin pregnancies is more contested and generally less favorable than for singletons.

Twins and a Short Cervix

Progesterone: An updated individual patient data meta-analysis in twin pregnancies with a cervical length of 25 mm or less (Romero et al., 2017, Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology) reported reductions in preterm birth before 30 to 35 weeks, neonatal death (RR 0.53; 95% CI 0.35–0.81), and neonatal morbidity, with no differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes at 4–5 years. One study contributed about 74% of the total sample, and ACOG Practice Bulletin 234 states that, regardless of cervical length, data do not definitively support vaginal progesterone in twin pregnancy.
Cerclage: Berghella (2005) found more preterm birth with cerclage in twins (RR 2.15). ACOG, as summarised in American Family Physician, states cerclage is not beneficial in twins with a cervical length under 25 mm but may reduce preterm birth if the cervix is under 15 mm.
Pessary: A review by Hobbins concluded that, at the time of writing, there was no compelling evidence that progesterone, cerclage, or pessary prevented preterm birth in twins.

Australia and the United States: Guidance and Practice

Summary of National Guidance

United States (ACOG Practice Bulletin 234, 2021): Short cervix defined as under 25 mm at 16–24 weeks; vaginal progesterone recommended for asymptomatic singleton pregnancies with a short cervix and no prior preterm birth; serial transvaginal screening from 16 to 24 weeks for singleton pregnancies with prior spontaneous preterm birth; progesterone or cerclage options for prior preterm birth with a short cervix through shared decision-making; ultrasound-indicated cerclage of uncertain benefit without prior preterm birth unless the cervix is under 10 mm; pessary not proven.
Australia (Australasian Society for Ultrasound in Medicine, RANZCOG, Australian Preterm Birth Prevention Alliance): Cervical length measurement at the mid-pregnancy morphology scan; nightly vaginal progesterone (200 mg in current practice) for a short cervix, continued until 37 weeks; cerclage reserved for women with prior preterm birth or a very short cervix.
International (ISUOG 2022): Standardized transvaginal measurement technique, as described above.

Newnham et al. (2017, American Journal of Obstetrics and Gynecology) evaluated the first statewide, multifaceted preterm birth prevention program, introduced in Western Australia in mid-2014. The program comprised new clinical guidelines, an outreach program for health practitioners, a public health campaign, and a dedicated referral clinic, and included seven interventions such as universal cervical length measurement at the mid-pregnancy scan and appropriate use of vaginal progesterone. In the first full calendar year (2015), the rate of singleton preterm birth in the state fell by 7.6% and was lower than in any of the preceding six years. This was a before-and-after population study of a combined program; the authors state that further research is needed to determine the relative contribution of each intervention, so the result cannot be attributed to cervical length screening alone. The Australian Preterm Birth Prevention Alliance was formed in June 2018 to extend the approach nationally.

Practical Questions to Ask Your Care Provider

If a Short Cervix Has Been Found

About the measurement: Was it measured transvaginally? At what gestational age? Was it the shortest of at least three measurements? Is funneling present? Should it be repeated, and when?
About your risk profile: Is this a singleton or twin pregnancy? Have you had a previous spontaneous preterm birth, second-trimester loss, or cone biopsy/LEEP? These change which treatments have evidence behind them.
About treatment: What does the evidence show for the option being offered, in someone with your history and your measurement? If progesterone is offered, what dose and formulation, and until what gestation? If a cerclage or pessary is suggested, what is the reason in your specific case?
About monitoring and symptoms: How often will the cervix be re-measured? Which symptoms should prompt an immediate call, and who should you call out of hours?

Limits of the Evidence and Open Questions

Several honest limitations run through this literature. The optimal cervical length threshold and its dependence on gestational age, parity, and history are not settled — trials have used cutoffs from 15 to 25 mm. The optimal progesterone dose and formulation are not established. Head-to-head trials of progesterone against cerclage are lacking (Cochrane, 2017). The pessary evidence has moved from a strongly positive early trial (PECEP, open-label) to neutral and then negative results (Nicolaides 2016; TOPS 2023), with a fetal and neonatal mortality signal in TOPS that remains unexplained. Measurement in the mid-pregnancy scan has limited sensitivity — a short cervix is an important marker, but many preterm births occur in women whose cervix was not short (Owen 2001 reported a sensitivity of 19% at the initial scan in a high-risk cohort). And guidelines continue to differ on universal screening. Guidance is updated over time, so anyone making decisions about her own pregnancy should rely on current advice from her obstetrician or midwife rather than on any single summary.
