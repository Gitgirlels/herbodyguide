Stillbirth: The Science of Causes, Risk Factors and Prevention

Stillbirth — the death of a baby before birth, late enough in pregnancy that it is counted separately from miscarriage — is one of the most common and least discussed adverse outcomes in obstetrics. The United Nations estimates that about 1.9 million babies were stillborn worldwide in 2021, more than 5,000 every day. In high-income countries such as Australia and the United States the risk for any one pregnancy is low, but it has proved hard to reduce, and many stillbirths remain unexplained even after thorough investigation. This guide sets out what the published evidence shows: how stillbirth is defined (and why the definition changes the numbers), how the way societies have recorded and responded to it has changed, how common it is in Australia, the United States and worldwide, what the best studies say about its causes and risk factors, which prevention strategies have been tested in trials and what those trials found, and where the evidence is still weak. It also covers the psychological and social effects on families, because the evidence on grief is part of the evidence on stillbirth. A note on tone: the subject is painful, and many readers will have a personal connection to it. Statistics describe populations, not individual families, and a stillbirth is never evidence that a parent did something wrong.

What Is Stillbirth? Definitions That Change the Numbers

There Is No Single Worldwide Definition

Stillbirth: A baby born with no signs of life after a threshold of pregnancy duration or birth weight. The threshold differs between countries, and this matters whenever rates are compared. The World Health Organization definition used for international comparison counts deaths at 28 completed weeks of pregnancy or later (or a birth weight of 1000 g or more), and the United Nations Never Forgotten report (2023) uses this 28-week definition for its global estimates. Individual countries often count earlier losses.
Australia: The Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW) counts stillbirths from 20 completed weeks of gestation or a birth weight of at least 400 g. Terminations of pregnancy at or after 20 weeks (or 400 g) are included in the standard rate. Using the WHO definition instead gives much lower figures: AIHW reports 2.9 per 1,000 births for 2022 on the WHO definition, compared with 8.0 per 1,000 on the national definition.
United States: The National Center for Health Statistics reports fetal deaths at 20 weeks of gestation or more. It separates early fetal deaths (20–27 weeks) from late fetal deaths (28 weeks or more).
United Kingdom: Since 1 October 1992, a stillbirth in England and Wales has been a baby born dead at 24 or more completed weeks; before that, the threshold was 28 weeks.
Miscarriage: Pregnancy loss before the stillbirth threshold in the relevant country. The dividing line is legal and statistical, not a biological boundary, which is why a loss at 22 weeks can be a stillbirth in Australia and the United States but not in the UK.

Why this matters: A headline rate of 8.0 per 1,000 in Australia and a global rate of 13.9 per 1,000 cannot be compared directly, because they use different gestational thresholds. The AIHW rate includes deaths from 20 weeks; the UN rate counts deaths from 28 weeks. When you see two stillbirth rates, the first question to ask is "from what gestational age?"
Analogy: Imagine counting "late arrivals" at work, but one office counts anyone after 9:00 am and another counts anyone after 9:30 am. The second office will report fewer late arrivals even if the staff behave identically. Stillbirth rates work the same way: the number depends on where the counting line is drawn.

Timing: Antepartum and Intrapartum Stillbirth

Researchers distinguish stillbirths that occur before labour begins (antepartum) from those that occur during labour (intrapartum). The distinction matters because the causes and the prevention strategies are different. The UN Never Forgotten report estimates that two in five stillbirths worldwide occur during labour. The 2011 Lancet Stillbirths Series estimated that about 1.2 million stillbirths in 2009, roughly 45% of the total, occurred during birth, most linked to obstetric emergencies, and noted that intrapartum stillbirth has been nearly eliminated in high-income countries. In countries like Australia and the United States, most stillbirths therefore occur before labour begins.

Stillbirth in Numbers

Worldwide

The UN Inter-agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME) report Never Forgotten (2023) estimated about 1.9 million stillbirths worldwide in 2021 (more than 5,000 per day), a global rate of 13.9 per 1,000 total births. That is down from 21.3 per 1,000 in 2000 — a 35% reduction — but it is a slower pace of progress (about 2.0% per year) than the roughly 4.0% annual reduction in under-five mortality over the same period. About 53 million stillbirths occurred worldwide between 2000 and 2021. Sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia together account for three-quarters of all stillbirths. Sub-Saharan Africa's rate was 21.0 per 1,000 in 2021, and its share of the global total rose from 26% in 2000 to 45% in 2021. The lowest rate — 2.9 per 1,000 — was in Europe, Northern America, Australia and New Zealand combined. Six countries (India, Pakistan, Nigeria, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Ethiopia and Bangladesh) account for almost half of all stillbirths.

Global Targets and What Reaching Them Would Mean

The Every Newborn Action Plan target is for every country to reach 12 or fewer stillbirths per 1,000 total births by 2030. The Never Forgotten report estimates that 56 countries need to accelerate progress to meet it, and that meeting the target worldwide could avert 2.6 million stillbirths before 2030. It also estimates that an aspirational goal of 3 per 1,000 (about the average in high-income countries) could save 8.4 million lives. The report states that most stillbirths can be prevented with access to good-quality antenatal and delivery care.

Australia

The AIHW reported 2,380 stillbirths among births in 2022 (77% of the 3,076 perinatal deaths that year), a rate of 8.0 per 1,000 births. This was up from 7.2 per 1,000 in 2021 (2,278 stillbirths) and was the highest rate in the series that begins in 2003 (7.1). Between those years the rate fluctuated, with a low of 6.7 in 2016 and a high of 7.7 in 2020. The AIHW cautions that part of the 2022 increase reflects improved case capture in some areas, so the trend should be interpreted with care. In 2022 the stillbirth rate was 9.6 per 1,000 in the most socioeconomically disadvantaged fifth of areas and 6.0 per 1,000 in the least disadvantaged fifth; the most disadvantaged group had the highest rate in every year from 2017 to 2022. Most of Australia's stillbirths occur earlier in pregnancy: the 2022 third-trimester stillbirth rate was 2.7 per 1,000 births, and the rate for stillbirths from 28 weeks in 2021 was 2.4 per 1,000.

Who Is Affected: Perinatal Death Rates in Australia, 2021

AIHW reports perinatal death (stillbirths and neonatal deaths combined) rates for 2021 by group. These are combined rates, so they are not stillbirth rates alone, but they show where the burden falls:
Mother's age: 21 per 1,000 births for mothers under 20; 14 per 1,000 for mothers aged 40 and over
First Nations mothers: 17 per 1,000 births
Very remote areas: 19 per 1,000 births
Most disadvantaged socioeconomic area: 12 per 1,000 births
Plurality: 8.7 per 1,000 for singletons, 35 per 1,000 for twins and 80 per 1,000 for triplets or higher. Complications specific to multiple pregnancy were the most common cause of stillbirth in multiple births (58%)

United States

The National Center for Health Statistics reported a fetal mortality rate of 5.73 per 1,000 live births and fetal deaths in 2021 (fetal deaths at 20 weeks or more), falling to 5.45 per 1,000 in 2022 — a 5% decline. Early fetal deaths (20–27 weeks) fell 6% and late fetal deaths (28 weeks or more) fell 4%. The decline was statistically significant for White non-Hispanic women (down 8%) and Hispanic women (down 5%); there was no significant change for other race and Hispanic-origin groups, so the gap between groups did not narrow in that year.
Reading these numbers together: Australia (8.0 per 1,000 from 20 weeks, 2022) and the United States (5.45 per 1,000 from 20 weeks, 2022) use similar gestational thresholds, but they are not identical systems: Australian figures include terminations at or after 20 weeks, and reporting practices differ. Both countries sit far below the global rate of 13.9 per 1,000 (28 weeks or more), but, as the Australian trend shows, rates in high-income countries have not fallen steadily.

How Understanding of Stillbirth Developed: A Timeline

Key Milestones in Recording, Research and Response

1750–1824: Sextons' burial books at the parish of St Martin-in-the-Fields in London record stillborn and "abortive" burials separately from other infants. Historians of the period find that stillborn and abortive burials equalled more than 20% of live-born infant burials, that stillbirth rates fluctuated widely, and that there was no convincing decline until after about 1810 — even as early neonatal mortality fell. A fee schedule from about 1725 charged less to bury a stillborn child than other infants, which shows that stillbirth was already treated as a distinct category.
Late 18th century: Records from a London workhouse suggest the stillbirth rate among workhouse births rose from 73 per 1,000 births (1760–73) to 104 per 1,000 (1784–99), and show that the boundary between "stillborn" and early infant death was applied inconsistently — some infants recorded as dying within one to three days were buried as stillborn.
1926: The Births and Deaths Registration Act 1926 sets out the legal definition of a stillborn child in England and Wales. Until 1992 the definition rested on the twenty-eighth week of pregnancy.
1958–1963: The British Perinatal Mortality Survey (Butler and Bonham) studies every birth in Great Britain in the week of 3–9 March 1958, with questionnaires returned for about 98% of notified births, and extends mortality analysis across March to May 1958. The first report, published in 1963, shows that perinatal mortality rose with parity and with social class (from class I to class V), and documents the contribution of congenital malformation and intrapartum asphyxia. It is one of the first large national studies to show that stillbirth and perinatal death follow social patterns.
1977: WHO definitions adopted with the ninth revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9) recommend that countries report all fetuses and infants of 500 g or more nationally, but that only those of 1000 g or more (or 28 completed weeks where weight is unknown) be included in statistics for international comparison.
Late 1970s: Parents in the United Kingdom organise. The charity now called Sands is founded by bereaved parents (Sands' own history gives 1978 in its introduction and 1977 in its milestone timeline). It was founded by psychiatric social worker Hazelanne Lewis after journalist Bel Mooney wrote about her own stillbirth in The Guardian and many parents responded. Its first campaign (1978) changes the stillbirth certificate from one for disposal to one for burial.
1982: Following parent campaigning, bereaved parents win the right to have their baby's name entered on the stillbirth registration certificate in the UK.
1992: The UK Still-Birth (Definition) Act 1992 (Royal Assent 16 March 1992) lowers the legal threshold from 28 to 24 completed weeks, effective 1 October 1992. The Office for National Statistics notes that stillbirth figures from 1993 are not fully comparable with earlier years.
1993: Sands introduces the teardrop sticker scheme to alert hospital staff, through the medical notes, that a parent has experienced a loss.
2009–2010: Sands publishes its Saving Babies' Lives report (2009) and, after its "Seeing and Holding" campaign, the guidance of the UK National Institute for Health and Care Excellence changes so that parents are offered a choice about seeing and holding their baby (2010).
2011: The Lancet publishes its first Stillbirths Series. It estimates 2.64 million stillbirths worldwide in 2009 (down from 3.03 million in 1995), finds that 98% occur in low- and middle-income countries, and challenges the view that stillbirth is unavoidable: congenital abnormalities account for under 5% of global stillbirths, and conditions such as syphilis are readily treatable. A meta-analysis in the series (Flenady et al.) identifies major risk factors in high-income countries.
2011: The US Stillbirth Collaborative Research Network (SCRN) publishes its study of causes of death among stillbirths, using standardised evaluation including postmortem examination, in JAMA.
2014: The Every Newborn Action Plan sets the target of 12 or fewer stillbirths per 1,000 total births in every country by 2030.
2016: The Lancet publishes its second series, Ending Preventable Stillbirths — five papers developed by 216 experts from more than 100 organisations in 43 countries. It estimates more than 2.6 million stillbirths per year (about 7,200 per day) and shows that stillbirth had fallen by only 2.0% per year, compared with 3.0% for maternal deaths and 4.5% for child deaths.
2018: The Australian Senate Select Committee on Stillbirth Research and Education, established in early 2018, receives more than 269 submissions, holds hearings in six locations, and tables its report on 4 December 2018 with 16 recommendations. The government announces an initial $7.2 million package the next day.
2018: The AFFIRM trial in the UK and Ireland reports that a package to raise awareness of reduced fetal movements did not reduce stillbirth.
2019: The Australian Government responds to the Senate report (July 2019), agreeing to three recommendations and agreeing in principle to the other thirteen, and commits to a National Stillbirth Action and Implementation Plan. A pooled analysis of case-control studies (Cronin et al.) reports that going to sleep supine is associated with higher risk of late stillbirth than going to sleep on the side.
2020: The Cochrane review of induction of labour at or beyond 37 weeks (Middleton et al.) finds high-certainty evidence of fewer perinatal deaths and stillbirths with a policy of induction. On 10 December 2020 Australia launches the National Stillbirth Action and Implementation Plan, with a target of reducing the stillbirth rate by 20% within five years.
2021: A US CDC report finds that stillbirth was more common among deliveries with a COVID-19 diagnosis (1.26%) than without (0.64%), with a much larger difference during the period when the Delta variant predominated.
2023: UN IGME publishes Never Forgotten with the 2021 estimates (1.9 million stillbirths; 13.9 per 1,000). The US NCHS reports a 5% decline in fetal mortality between 2021 and 2022.

What Causes Stillbirth?

Stillbirth Is a Group of Conditions, Not One Disease

There is no single cause of stillbirth. The underlying processes include problems with how the placenta works, problems with the baby, problems in the mother's health, infection, and problems with the umbilical cord or with labour. In many cases more than one factor contributes, and in a substantial proportion no cause can be found. The proportions vary greatly with the country, the definition, the gestational age and how thoroughly the death is investigated.

The most complete investigation of causes in a high-income country is the US Stillbirth Collaborative Research Network (SCRN) study (JAMA, 2011). It examined 512 stillbirths; 500 women consented to postmortem examination. The researchers identified a possible or probable cause of death in 390 stillbirths (76.2%; 95% confidence interval 72.2–79.8%), and a probable cause in 312 (60.9%). The categories identified were: obstetric complications (29.3%), placental abnormalities (23.6%), fetal genetic or structural abnormalities (13.7%), infection (12.9%), umbilical cord abnormalities (10.4%), hypertensive disorders (9.2%) and other maternal medical conditions (7.8%). These percentages add to more than 100%, which means that the categories overlap — one stillbirth can have more than one identified contributing cause.
What the SCRN result teaches: A cause was found for more than three-quarters of stillbirths when the evaluation was thorough (including postmortem examination and placental assessment). Where investigation is partial, far more stillbirths are labelled "unexplained". "Unexplained" therefore often means "not fully investigated" rather than "unknowable".

Placental Dysfunction and Fetal Growth Restriction

The placenta carries oxygen and nutrients to the baby. When it does not function well, the baby may grow slowly (fetal growth restriction, FGR) and, in some cases, die. A population-based study in an English region (Gardosi et al., BMJ, 2013) examined 92,218 normally formed singleton pregnancies delivered in 2009–11, among which there were 389 stillbirths from 24 weeks (4.2 per 1,000 births). Half of these stillbirths (195 of 389, 50.1%) were of babies with FGR, defined as a birth weight below the 10th customised centile — and in 160 of them (82%) the growth restriction had not been detected before birth. Fetal growth restriction raised the risk of stillbirth about 7.8-fold in non-smokers (adjusted relative risk 7.8; 95% CI 5.6–10.9) and accounted for 22.2% of stillbirths in the model. The study highlights two things: that FGR is a major pathway to stillbirth, and that detection before birth was the weak link in that population.

Placental Separation and Bleeding

Bleeding before labour (antepartum haemorrhage), including placental abruption (when the placenta separates from the uterine wall), is a recognised cause of stillbirth. In the Gardosi study, antepartum haemorrhage carried an adjusted relative risk of 3.4 (95% CI 2.6–4.5) and a population-attributable risk of 15.5%. Because abruption is often sudden, it is among the causes that are hardest to prevent through screening.

Fetal Genetic and Structural Conditions

In the SCRN study 13.7% of stillbirths had a fetal genetic or structural abnormality identified. This is a smaller share than many people assume, and the 2011 Lancet series found that congenital abnormalities account for under 5% of stillbirths worldwide, because in low- and middle-income countries other causes (infection, labour complications) dominate.

Infection

Infection was identified in 12.9% of stillbirths in the SCRN study. Globally, infection is a larger contributor in regions where malaria and syphilis are common. The 2016 Lancet series estimated that malaria accounts for 8.0% of stillbirths worldwide (20.0% in sub-Saharan Africa) and syphilis for 7.7% worldwide (11.2% in sub-Saharan Africa) — both of which are preventable or treatable. The same series estimated that at least 10% of stillbirths are linked to each of diabetes and hypertension.

Hypertensive Disorders, Diabetes and Other Maternal Conditions

Hypertensive disorders (9.2%) and other maternal medical conditions (7.8%) were identified in the SCRN study. A meta-analysis of risk factors in high-income countries (Flenady et al., Lancet, 2011) found strong associations between stillbirth and pre-existing diabetes and pre-existing hypertension, discussed below.

Umbilical Cord Problems and Labour Complications

Umbilical cord abnormalities were identified in 10.4% of stillbirths in the SCRN study and obstetric complications in 29.3%. The latter category includes conditions such as placental abruption, multiple-pregnancy complications and complications of labour. In multiple pregnancies, AIHW reports that complications specific to multiple pregnancy account for most stillbirths (58% in Australia in 2021).

Risk Factors: What Raises the Chance of Stillbirth?

Putting Relative Risk in Context

Almost every pregnancy that ends in stillbirth involves a woman who has no obvious risk factor, and most women with a risk factor have a healthy baby. Relative risk figures describe how much a factor shifts the odds compared with women without it; they do not say how likely stillbirth is for any individual. When absolute risks are low, even a doubled risk remains small in absolute terms.

Analogy: Think of a lottery in which each ticket has a very small chance of winning. A risk factor is like buying a second ticket: your chance of "winning" doubles, but it is still a very small chance. That is why risk factors are used to guide extra monitoring and advice — not to predict which pregnancies will end in stillbirth.
A systematic review and meta-analysis of 96 population-based studies in high-income countries (Flenady et al., Lancet, 2011) estimated how much of the stillbirth burden is attributable to common risk factors. Population-attributable risks were reported as 8–18% for maternal overweight and obesity, 7–11% for maternal age over 35, 4–7% for smoking, about 15% for first pregnancy (primiparity) and 23% for the baby being small for gestational age. The review concluded that risk factors that are fully or partly avoidable account for a large share of stillbirths in high-income countries, and named overweight, obesity, maternal age and smoking as priority risk factors for prevention.

Maternal Weight

A dose-response meta-analysis of 38 cohort studies (Aune et al., JAMA, 2014) found that each 5-unit increase in maternal body mass index (BMI) was associated with a higher risk of stillbirth (relative risk 1.24; 95% CI 1.18–1.30). In absolute terms, the paper's model estimated about 40 stillbirths per 10,000 pregnancies at a BMI of 20 and about 59 per 10,000 at a BMI of 30. The authors noted that most of the included studies came from Europe and North America, so the results may not generalise to low- and middle-income countries.

Smoking

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 34 papers (Marufu et al., BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth, 2015) found that smoking during pregnancy was associated with a 47% increase in the odds of stillbirth (odds ratio 1.47; 95% CI 1.37–1.57). In the English regional study, active smoking without FGR carried an adjusted relative risk of 2.5 (95% CI 1.7–3.6), and the combined contribution of obesity, smoking and FGR was estimated at about 56% of stillbirths. Smoking is also one of the few risk factors that can change quickly: it is modifiable in a way that age or previous stillbirth is not.

Maternal Age, First Pregnancy, and Other Characteristics

Maternal age over 35 and a first pregnancy both contribute to the stillbirth burden in high-income countries (Flenady 2011, above). In the English regional study, first pregnancy carried an adjusted relative risk of 1.8 (95% CI 1.3–2.5), and women having their third or later baby had a relative risk of 1.6 (95% CI 1.0–2.5). Pre-existing diabetes was associated with a relative risk of 3.9 (95% CI 1.7–8.9) in the same study; BMI of 35 or more with 1.6 (95% CI 1.1–2.4); a history of mental health problems with 1.4 (95% CI 1.0–1.9). The Australian data show that very young mothers and mothers aged 40 and over both have elevated combined perinatal death rates compared with the average.

Social Disadvantage and Inequality

Stillbirth follows social patterns. The 1958 British survey documented a gradient with social class; the 2016 Lancet series reported that in high-income countries, women in the most disadvantaged communities face at least double the risk; and in Australia in 2022 the stillbirth rate was 9.6 per 1,000 in the most disadvantaged fifth of areas versus 6.0 per 1,000 in the least disadvantaged. In the United States, the 2021–2022 decline in fetal mortality was seen for White non-Hispanic and Hispanic women but not for other race and Hispanic-origin groups. The English regional study also found a relative risk of 3.0 (95% CI 1.9–4.8) for non-UK-born women of Pakistani ethnicity compared with the reference group, an example of how ethnic differences in stillbirth risk have been documented and are still being explained.

Multiple Pregnancy

In Australia in 2021, the perinatal death rate was 8.7 per 1,000 births for singletons, 35 per 1,000 for twins and 80 per 1,000 for triplets or higher. Complications of multiple pregnancy were the most common cause of stillbirth in multiples (58%).

A Previous Stillbirth

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 studies from high-income countries (Lamont et al., BMJ, 2015) found that women with a previous stillbirth had a recurrence risk of 2.5% (606 of 24,541) compared with 0.4% (13,677 of 3,387,538) in women without a previous stillbirth — about a fivefold increase before adjustment, and up to fourfold after adjusting for maternal age, smoking and deprivation. For most women the large majority of later pregnancies still end in a live birth. The authors recommended pre-pregnancy counselling, attention to modifiable factors such as smoking and obesity, close monitoring and prompt intervention when risk increases. They noted that evidence about recurrence after an unexplained stillbirth is limited.

Sleep Position in Late Pregnancy

An individual participant data meta-analysis of case-control studies (Cronin et al., EClinicalMedicine, 2019) pooled five studies with 851 cases of late stillbirth (28 weeks or more) and 2,257 controls. Going to sleep on the back (supine) was associated with higher odds of late stillbirth than going to sleep on the left side (adjusted odds ratio 2.63; 95% CI 1.72–4.04), while going to sleep on the right side was not different from the left side (adjusted odds ratio 1.04; 95% CI 0.83–1.31). The estimated population-attributable risk was 5.8% (95% CI 3.2–9.2%), which the authors interpreted as meaning that stillbirth might fall by about 5.8% if all women at or beyond 28 weeks settled to sleep on their side. They found no significant interaction with small-for-gestational-age babies, maternal obesity or smoking, and concluded that the supine position appears to be a risk factor in all pregnancies. This evidence comes from case-control studies, which cannot prove that sleep position causes stillbirth, because women were asked about sleep position after the event. It nonetheless forms the basis of the "side sleeping" advice now given in Australia.

COVID-19 During Pregnancy

A US CDC analysis (DeSisto et al., MMWR, 2021) of 1,249,634 delivery hospitalisations in 736 hospitals between March 2020 and September 2021 found stillbirth in 1.26% (273 of 21,653) of deliveries with a COVID-19 diagnosis and 0.64% (7,881 of 1,227,981) of those without; the adjusted relative risk was 1.90 (95% CI 1.69–2.15). The association was stronger when the Delta variant predominated (July–September 2021; adjusted relative risk 4.04; 95% CI 3.28–4.97) than before it (1.47; 95% CI 1.27–1.71). This is observational hospital data and can show an association; it cannot show how the infection might lead to stillbirth.

Symptoms and Warning Signs

Why Stillbirth Is Often Unannounced

Many stillbirths occur without a warning symptom that parents recognise. The most commonly discussed warning sign is a change in the baby's usual movements, which is the reason why fetal movement awareness has been studied as a prevention strategy (see below). Vaginal bleeding is another warning sign associated with higher risk (antepartum haemorrhage had an adjusted relative risk of 3.4 in the Gardosi study). Parents who notice a change in their baby's usual pattern of movement, or any bleeding, should contact their maternity unit or midwife straight away rather than waiting for the next scheduled appointment. Local guidance varies on how to monitor movements, and a pregnancy care team can explain what is usual for the individual pregnancy.

Important: Not noticing a warning sign is never a failure on the part of a parent. In the Gardosi study, four in five growth-restricted stillbirths were not detected before birth even though the women were under routine antenatal care. Neither parents nor clinicians recognised these babies as being at risk.

Prevention: What Have the Trials Shown?

Timing of Birth: Induction of Labour at or Beyond 37 Weeks

A Cochrane systematic review (Middleton et al., 2020) of 34 randomised trials involving over 21,000 women and infants compared a policy of labour induction at or beyond 37 weeks with expectant management (waiting for spontaneous labour, with monitoring). Induction was associated with fewer perinatal deaths (risk ratio 0.31; 95% CI 0.15–0.64; 22 trials, 18,795 infants; high-certainty evidence) — 4 perinatal deaths with induction versus 25 with expectant management — and fewer stillbirths (risk ratio 0.30; 95% CI 0.12–0.75; 2 stillbirths versus 16). Caesarean birth was probably less frequent with induction (risk ratio 0.90; 95% CI 0.85–0.95; moderate-certainty evidence). The authors note that the absolute numbers are small — about 0.4 versus 3 perinatal deaths per 1,000 — and that the best timing of induction and long-term childhood outcomes still need research.
Reading this result: The reduction is large in relative terms and small in absolute terms, because stillbirth near term is uncommon. The review does not mean that every woman should be induced at 37 weeks; it shows that induction policies reduce deaths on average in trial populations. Decisions about timing of birth are made individually, with the woman's own risk factors and preferences, and trial populations may differ from those of any one woman.

Awareness of Reduced Fetal Movements: The AFFIRM Trial

The AFFIRM trial (Norman et al., Lancet, 2018) was a stepped-wedge cluster-randomised trial in 33 maternity hospitals in the UK and Ireland, with 409,175 pregnancies. Hospitals were randomised to the dates when they introduced a care package that included raising women's awareness of the importance of reduced fetal movements and a standardised management protocol for women who reported them. The stillbirth rate was 4.40 per 1,000 births in the control period and 4.06 per 1,000 in the intervention period (adjusted odds ratio 0.90; 95% CI 0.75–1.07), a difference that could be due to chance. The authors concluded that the evidence did not support this package as a way to reduce stillbirth.

What the AFFIRM Result Does and Does Not Mean

The trial tested a specific package, applied across whole hospitals. It did not show that reporting reduced movements is unimportant, and women should still contact their maternity unit if they notice a change. It showed that this package, as delivered, did not measurably reduce stillbirth at population level. Trials of this type are hard to interpret in either direction, because even a package that worked would be diluted if only a minority of at-risk women changed their behaviour.

Multi-Component Care Bundles: The Australian Safer Baby Bundle

The Safer Baby Bundle is an Australian quality-improvement programme for maternity services, developed by the Stillbirth Centre of Research Excellence, which targets five elements: supporting women to stop smoking, detecting and managing fetal growth restriction, raising awareness of and responding to decreased fetal movements, advising side sleeping from 28 weeks, and informing decisions about timing of birth. The Australian Government committed to its national rollout in its 2019 response to the Senate inquiry.

A pre-post survey study published in BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth (2024) evaluated implementation of the Safer Baby Bundle in New South Wales and Queensland services, using surveys of 1,225 women (1,096 before and 129 after implementation) and 1,415 healthcare professionals (1,148 before and 267 after). After implementation, more healthcare professionals reported giving advice on side sleeping "all the time" (from 20.4% to 79.4%), and more women recalled conversations about reducing stillbirth risk (from 32.2% to 50.4%). Support for stopping smoking improved less (15.6% to 20.3%; not statistically significant, p = 0.088). The study did not measure stillbirth rates — its authors state that clinical outcome evaluations will be reported separately — and it had much lower response rates after implementation than before, with self-reported practice data.
What can and cannot be said: The published survey evidence shows that the bundle changed what clinicians reported doing and what women remembered being told. It does not by itself show that the bundle reduces stillbirth. That judgement depends on outcome data, and any reader should look for those separately.

Modifiable Risk Factors

Evidence-Based Areas for Prevention in High-Income Countries

Stopping smoking: Smoking carries an odds ratio of 1.47 for stillbirth (Marufu 2015), and is one of the priority modifiable factors identified by the 2011 Lancet analysis.
Weight before pregnancy: Each 5-unit increase in BMI is associated with a relative risk of 1.24 (Aune 2014). The authors recommended that weight management guidance for women planning pregnancy take this into account.
Sleep position: Settling to sleep on the side rather than the back in late pregnancy is associated with lower risk in pooled case-control data (Cronin 2019), though this is not trial evidence.
Detecting growth restriction: Because 82% of growth-restricted stillbirths in the English study were not detected before birth, better detection is an obvious target, though improved detection has to be matched by effective action once a baby is identified as small.
Timing of birth: High-certainty trial evidence shows fewer perinatal deaths with an induction policy at or beyond 37 weeks (Middleton 2020), with the caveats above.
Prevention in low- and middle-income countries: The 2016 Lancet series emphasised malaria prevention, syphilis screening and treatment, and care in labour, since these causes are common and largely preventable there.

After a Stillbirth: Investigation and Care

Why Investigation Matters

The SCRN study showed that a standardised evaluation, including postmortem examination of the baby, identified a probable or possible cause in more than three-quarters of stillbirths. Parents who want to know why their baby died are therefore more likely to receive an answer when a thorough evaluation is carried out. Investigation can also inform the care of later pregnancies. Policies and practice differ between Australia and the United States, and between hospitals; parents can ask their care team which investigations are available, what each can and cannot show, and how to consent to or decline them.

The Role of Respectful Bereavement Care

Bereavement care has changed because of campaigning by parents. In the UK, the Sands campaigns of the late 1970s through the 2010s led to changes including the stillbirth certificate (1978), the right to name a baby on the registration certificate (1982), the teardrop sticker scheme to alert staff (1993) and national guidance that parents be offered a choice about seeing and holding their baby (2010). In Australia, the government's 2019 response to the Senate inquiry included funding for Sands Australia to provide support to families.

Grief, Mental Health and Social Effects

The 2016 Lancet series paper on economic and psychosocial consequences (Heazell et al.) concluded that the consequences of stillbirth are overlooked and underappreciated, that data on direct costs are sparse but suggest that a stillbirth needs more resources than a live birth, that indirect and intangible costs are extensive and are usually met by families alone, and that negative effects on parental mental health might be moderated by empathic attitudes of care providers. Reporting on the series, the University of Manchester noted estimates that at least 4.2 million women worldwide have symptoms of depression due to stillbirth, often lasting years; that among 3,503 parents surveyed in high-income countries almost half felt that society wanted them to forget their baby and have another child; that about 10% of bereaved parents stay off work for six months; and that the direct financial costs of stillbirth care are 10–70% higher than those of a live birth.
A systematic review and meta-summary of 144 studies (Burden et al., BMC Pregnancy and Childbirth, 2016) described the psychosocial impact of stillbirth. The most frequent theme was negative psychological symptoms after the loss (77.1% of studies), followed by disenfranchised grief — grief that is not socially acknowledged — (31.2%), grief that differs between partners (incongruent grief, 28.5%), psychological symptoms in later pregnancies (27.1%), effects on siblings (23.6%) and avoidance of memories (13.2%). Among fathers, suppression of grief (18.1%) was commonly described; in low- and middle-income countries, stigma was a theme in 13.2% of studies. The review also found some positive changes: changed views on life, death and identity (25.7%) and a motivation to improve healthcare (4.2%). The authors described stillbirth as a life-changing event with severe psychological, physical and social costs, with effects on later relationships and subsequent children.
If this is your experience: Grief after stillbirth is a normal response to a profound loss, and it is not a sign of weakness or something to "get over" on a timetable. Many parents find that specialist bereavement organisations, such as Sands in the UK and Australia, peer support, and perinatal mental health services help. If grief is accompanied by thoughts of self-harm or if you feel unsafe, contact your doctor or local emergency services straight away.

Australia and the United States: Policy and Practice

Australia: A National Response

Australia has built one of the most developed national responses to stillbirth. After the Senate inquiry's December 2018 report, the Australian Government's July 2019 response agreed with three of its 16 recommendations (including a National Stillbirth Action Plan, a national public awareness campaign and stillbirth provisions in workplace law) and agreed in principle with the remaining thirteen. It announced an initial $7.2 million package on 5 December 2018 and committed funding for a Stillbirth Centre of Research Excellence, national rollout of the Safer Baby Bundle and support for families through Sands Australia. The National Stillbirth Action and Implementation Plan, launched on 10 December 2020, set a goal of reducing the stillbirth rate by 20% within five years and of ensuring families receive respectful bereavement care. The most recent AIHW data show that the national rate has not fallen steadily: it was 8.0 per 1,000 in 2022, the highest in the series since 2003, with the caveat that improved case capture contributed to that figure. Judging whether the plan has worked will require several years of consistent data.

United States: Data, Disparities and Progress

The United States reports fetal deaths at 20 weeks or more through the National Vital Statistics System. The 2021–2022 data show a 5% decline in the overall rate (5.73 to 5.45 per 1,000), but a decline that was concentrated among White non-Hispanic and Hispanic women, not among other race and Hispanic-origin groups. The SCRN, a US National Institutes of Health-funded network, produced the most thorough US data on causes of stillbirth (2011). The US COVID-19 findings (DeSisto 2021) show how a new exposure can change risk in the population within a short period.

Practical Questions to Ask Your Care Provider

Questions That Come From the Evidence

"What are my personal risk factors, and which can I change?" Smoking, weight before pregnancy and pre-existing conditions such as diabetes and hypertension all appear in the evidence above.
"How will you check my baby's growth?" Because most growth-restricted stillbirths were not detected before birth in the English study, it is reasonable to ask how growth will be monitored and what will happen if a baby is measuring small.
"What should I do if my baby's movements change?" Ask for the local advice and the number to call, day or night.
"Which sleeping position do you advise in late pregnancy?" The pooled evidence suggests that settling to sleep on either side is associated with lower risk than going to sleep on the back.
"When do you recommend that the baby is born, and why?" Timing is individual and depends on risk factors, so ask about the reasoning and the options.
"If I have had a stillbirth before, what extra care can I have?" Recurrence risk is higher, though most later pregnancies end well, and pre-pregnancy counselling is recommended by the authors of the main meta-analysis.

Limits of the Evidence and Open Questions

What We Still Do Not Know

Comparability: Different gestational thresholds mean that national rates are not directly comparable, and changes in definitions (such as the UK change in 1992) break historical series.
Changing rates in high-income countries: Stillbirth rates in high-income countries have shown little or no improvement over two decades according to the 2011 analysis, and Australia's rate in 2022 was the highest in its series. Whether recent programmes will change this is not yet known.
Sleep position: The strongest evidence is from case-control studies. A causal link has not been shown in a randomised trial, and such a trial would be difficult to run.
Fetal movement awareness: The one large randomised trial found no significant benefit of the package it tested, so the best way to use movement awareness to prevent stillbirth is uncertain.
Induction: The review shows fewer deaths with induction policies, but the absolute numbers of deaths in the trials are small, the best timing is still uncertain, and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes have not been reported in any trial.
Care bundles: The published Safer Baby Bundle survey shows changes in practice and communication, but the evidence about stillbirth rates comes from separate outcome evaluations that readers should consult directly.
Unexplained stillbirth: The proportion varies with how thoroughly deaths are investigated. Better classification and more consistent investigation would make research and comparisons more reliable.
Observational data: Studies of risk factors and of COVID-19 are observational. They show associations, not proof of cause.

Bottom line: Stillbirth is uncommon in high-income countries, but it is not rare enough to ignore. The best evidence points to a handful of actions that can lower risk on average: not smoking, a healthy weight before pregnancy, good detection and management of growth restriction, going to sleep on the side in late pregnancy, timely care when something changes, and individually planned timing of birth. None of these guarantees a live birth, and none of them is a measure of whether a parent has done enough. Many stillbirths happen in pregnancies with no identifiable risk factor, and families who experience one deserve investigation, support and compassion.
