Vaginal Discharge: The Science of Normal and Abnormal Vaginal Secretions

```
Vaginal discharge is one of the most common reasons women seek gynaecological care, yet it remains one of the most poorly understood aspects of female physiology. Normal discharge is not a sign of illness — it is an active, hormonally regulated secretion that protects the reproductive tract, facilitates fertilisation, and reflects the health of the vaginal ecosystem. This comprehensive guide examines the biology of vaginal secretions from the ground up: the anatomy producing them, the microbiome that shapes them, the hormonal forces that drive their cyclical changes, and the evidence-based framework for distinguishing physiological variation from pathology.

Anatomy of Discharge Production: Where Does It Come From?

The Vaginal Wall and Transudation

The vagina itself has no secretory glands. Yet it contributes substantially to vaginal discharge through a process called transudation — the passive movement of fluid across its walls.

Vaginal Transudation

The vaginal epithelium is a stratified squamous mucosa supplied by a rich submucosal vascular plexus. Plasma filtrate continuously seeps across this epithelium from capillaries into the vaginal lumen, carrying water, electrolytes, proteins, and immunological components. The rate of transudation increases dramatically with sexual arousal — the physiological basis of vaginal lubrication — but basal transudation is continuous, providing the moisture that maintains vaginal epithelial health and contributes to the fluid environment of normal discharge.

The Cervix: The Major Secretory Source

The cervix is the primary source of secreted material in vaginal discharge under normal, non-aroused conditions. Its contribution changes dramatically across the menstrual cycle and across a woman's reproductive lifespan.

Endocervical Glands and Mucus Production

The endocervical canal is lined with columnar epithelium containing specialised mucus-secreting crypts. These glands produce 20–60 mg of cervical mucus daily during the follicular phase, rising to 600 mg or more at mid-cycle ovulation. Cervical mucus is a complex hydrogel composed of water (90–98%), mucin glycoproteins (predominantly MUC5B), electrolytes, enzymes, immunoglobulins (especially secretory IgA), and antimicrobial peptides including defensins and lactoferrin. Its physical and biochemical properties are exquisitely sensitive to estrogen and progesterone, changing across the menstrual cycle in ways that either facilitate or impede sperm transport.

The transformation zone — the area on the cervix where the columnar endocervical epithelium meets the squamous ectocervical epithelium — is the most biologically active region of the cervix and the site where most cervical pathology, including HPV-related changes, originates. In younger women and during pregnancy, this zone is often everted and visible on the ectocervix (cervical ectropion), where the exposed columnar cells produce visibly more mucus — a common, normal finding that is frequently misinterpreted as pathological discharge.

Other Contributing Sources

Several additional structures contribute to the fluid environment of the vaginal tract, and awareness of them helps contextualise the variation in normal discharge.

Bartholin's Glands, Skene's Glands, and the Endometrium

Bartholin's glands are a pair of pea-sized glands located at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions at the vaginal introitus. They secrete a small amount of mucus under sexual arousal, contributing to introital lubrication, but their resting secretion is minimal.

Skene's glands (paraurethral glands), located adjacent to the distal urethra, produce a small continuous secretion and are considered the female homologue of the prostate. Their contribution to basal vaginal fluid is minor but detectable by proteomics.

Endometrial and tubal fluid descends through the cervical canal and joins the vaginal secretion, carrying shed endometrial cells, leukocytes, and proteins — particularly in the periovulatory and perimenstrual phases.

Normal Discharge: Composition and Physiological Range

What Normal Discharge Actually Is

Normal vaginal discharge — clinically termed physiological leukorrhea — is a mixture of cervical mucus, vaginal transudation fluid, desquamated epithelial cells, and the metabolic byproducts of the vaginal microbiome. Understanding its composition helps demystify the wide range of appearances that fall within normal.

Cellular and Biochemical Composition

Microscopy of normal vaginal discharge reveals large numbers of superficial and intermediate squamous epithelial cells shed continuously from the vaginal walls. The density of epithelial shedding is driven by estrogen — higher estrogen levels produce thicker, more glycogen-rich epithelium with faster cellular turnover. Lactobacilli (discussed in detail below) are the dominant organisms visible on microscopy in healthy reproductive-age women. White blood cells (leukocytes) are present in small numbers but should not exceed approximately one per epithelial cell in normal discharge. The biochemical environment is characterised by low pH (3.8–4.5), moderate protein content, and the lactic acid and hydrogen peroxide produced by resident Lactobacillus species.

Population studies using daily sampling demonstrate that the average volume of vaginal discharge is approximately 1–4 mL per 24 hours in reproductive-age women, though this varies considerably across the menstrual cycle, between individuals, and with hormonal status. Importantly, there is no universal "normal" volume — what matters is consistency with an individual's baseline rather than any absolute threshold.

Normal Colour, Consistency, and Odour

A common source of anxiety for women is uncertainty about whether the characteristics of their discharge fall within a normal range. The scientific literature provides a reasonably clear framework for what to expect.

The Vaginal Microbiome: The Hidden Architecture of Discharge

Lactobacillus Dominance and the Healthy Vaginal Ecosystem

The vaginal microbiome is one of the most distinctive microbial ecosystems in the human body. Unlike most body sites — including the gut — where high microbial diversity is considered a marker of health, a healthy vaginal microbiome is characterised by low diversity and dominance of a single genus.

The Four Dominant Lactobacillus Species

Metagenomic studies have identified four Lactobacillus species as the principal colonisers of the healthy vagina: L. crispatus, L. iners, L. jensenii, and L. gasseri. Of these, L. crispatus-dominant communities are associated with the lowest vaginal pH, highest hydrogen peroxide production, and the greatest resistance to infection and dysbiosis — making L. crispatus dominance the gold standard of vaginal microbiome health. L. iners is the most prevalent species overall but confers less protection, is more metabolically flexible, and appears more likely to persist through transitions to dysbiosis.

Landmark metagenomic studies of over 400 North American women identified five Community State Types (CSTs) of vaginal microbiome composition. CST I (L. crispatus-dominant), II (L. iners-dominant), III (L. jensenii-dominant), and V (L. gasseri-dominant) are considered healthy. CST IV is characterised by low Lactobacillus abundance and high diversity of anaerobic bacteria — the microbiome signature of bacterial vaginosis. Notably, the prevalence of CST IV varies substantially by ethnicity: approximately 40% of Black women and 30% of Hispanic women harbour CST IV compared to 15% of white and Asian women, a disparity with significant public health implications.

How Lactobacilli Protect the Vagina

The protective role of vaginal Lactobacillus species is mediated through several intersecting mechanisms that collectively create a hostile environment for pathogenic bacteria, fungi, and viruses.

Lactic Acid and Vaginal Acidification

Lactobacilli metabolise glycogen released from desquamating vaginal epithelial cells, producing lactic acid as the primary fermentation product. This maintains vaginal pH at 3.8–4.5 — an environment that inhibits the growth of most bacterial and fungal pathogens. Both D- and L-lactic acid isomers are produced, and evidence suggests D-lactic acid (produced predominantly by L. crispatus) has superior antimicrobial and antiviral properties, including activity against HIV. pH elevation above 4.5 is among the most sensitive clinical indicators of disrupted vaginal health.

Hydrogen Peroxide, Bacteriocins, and Competitive Exclusion

Many Lactobacillus strains produce hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), which has direct antimicrobial activity and acts synergistically with cervical peroxidases and halides to generate potent oxidising agents against pathogens. Lactobacilli also produce bacteriocins — small proteinaceous antimicrobial peptides — that inhibit the growth of competing bacteria. Through competitive exclusion, Lactobacillus species physically occupy adhesion sites on the vaginal epithelium, preventing pathogen attachment. This combination of chemical and competitive defences constitutes a highly effective innate immune system at the vaginal mucosa.

Factors That Disrupt the Vaginal Microbiome

The Lactobacillus-dominant community state is dynamic and can be disrupted by multiple exogenous and endogenous factors. Understanding these allows women to make informed choices about vaginal hygiene and health practices.

Known Disruptors of the Vaginal Microbiome

Vaginal douching: Removes protective Lactobacillus communities, elevates pH, and is one of the strongest behavioural predictors of bacterial vaginosis and STI acquisition. No evidence supports douching as a hygiene practice.

Antibiotics: Broad-spectrum antibiotics — particularly those targeting anaerobes — can deplete vaginal Lactobacillus populations. Microbiome recovery typically takes 4–8 weeks, during which risk of vulvovaginal candidiasis and BV is elevated.

Semen: Semen is alkaline (pH 7.2–8.0) and contains prostaglandins that temporarily suppress vaginal Lactobacillus colonisation. Studies show measurable vaginal microbiome disruption for 24–48 hours following unprotected intercourse.

Menstruation: Menstrual blood is alkaline and transiently raises vaginal pH, with studies showing brief decreases in Lactobacillus relative abundance during and immediately after menstruation.

Hormonal changes: Declining estrogen at menopause reduces glycogen availability, leading to Lactobacillus depletion, rising pH, and the characteristic changes of genitourinary syndrome of menopause.

Scented products: Scented soaps, washes, and sprays applied intravaginally disrupt pH and microbiome composition. External use of gentle, unscented soap is all that is required for vulval hygiene.

The Cervical Cycle: How Discharge Changes Across the Menstrual Cycle

Follicular Phase: Building Toward Ovulation

The cyclical changes in vaginal discharge across the menstrual cycle are driven primarily by the changing estrogen-to-progesterone ratio and are among the most reliable indicators of ovarian function.

Days 1–5: Menstruation

During menstruation, the dominant vaginal content is menstrual blood and shed endometrium, obscuring typical discharge characteristics. As menstrual flow lightens in the final days, a small amount of white or cloudy discharge may be noted as cervical secretion resumes.

Days 6–11: Early and Mid-Follicular Phase

As rising follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) drives follicular development and estrogen levels begin to climb, cervical mucus production increases progressively. Discharge during this phase is typically scanty and opaque — white or pale yellow, with a sticky or tacky texture. The mucin network during this phase is dense and disorganised, providing an effective barrier to sperm.

Days 12–14: Late Follicular and Periovulatory Phase

The periovulatory surge in estrogen (driven by the dominant follicle approaching peak maturity) transforms cervical mucus dramatically. Under high estrogen stimulation, the cervix secretes large volumes of mucus with dramatically increased water content, reduced mucin concentration, and a highly organised parallel microfibrillar structure. This produces the characteristic raw egg-white appearance — clear, slippery, highly elastic (spinnbarkeit: stretching >6 cm without breaking), and with minimal odour. A classic clinical sign, the "fern pattern" visible on dried cervical mucus under microscopy, results from this crystalline mucin organisation. This is the biologically optimal environment for sperm transport and survival.

Studies using quantitative cervical mucus scoring (the Billings and Creighton methods) have validated the periovulatory mucus pattern as a reliable indicator of the fertile window. The probability of conception on days of peak cervical mucus (clear, slippery, elastic) is approximately 3-fold higher than on days of sticky or absent mucus, making mucus observation a useful component of fertility awareness-based methods of family planning.

Days 15–28: Luteal Phase

Following ovulation, the corpus luteum produces progesterone, which rapidly transforms cervical mucus back to an opaque, viscous, non-elastic state within 24–48 hours. The mucin network becomes dense and randomly organised, forming an impenetrable plug that excludes sperm and microorganisms from the upper reproductive tract. Women often notice a return to sticky, creamy, or absent discharge in the luteal phase. A small increase in more fluid, whitish discharge in the final days before menstruation is common and reflects the progesterone withdrawal phase.

Discharge Changes Across the Lifespan

The characteristics of vaginal discharge change substantially at different life stages, reflecting the shifting hormonal environment at each phase.

Neonates

Female newborns frequently have a small amount of white mucus or even blood-tinged discharge in the first 1–2 weeks of life. This is caused by withdrawal of maternal estrogen — to which the fetal cervix and vagina were exposed in utero — and is entirely benign. It resolves spontaneously within two weeks.

Childhood and Pre-Puberty

In the absence of significant estrogen stimulation, prepubertal girls have thin, atrophic vaginal epithelium with minimal glycogen and sparse Lactobacillus colonisation. Vaginal pH is neutral to slightly alkaline (6.5–7.5). Any notable discharge in prepubertal girls warrants careful evaluation to exclude vulvovaginitis (commonly caused by non-specific bacteria or poor hygiene), foreign bodies, or — when appropriate — the possibility of sexual abuse.

Perimenopause and Menopause

As ovarian estrogen production declines, vaginal epithelium thins, glycogen content falls, and Lactobacillus populations diminish. The resulting genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) — previously called vaginal atrophy — is characterised by reduced discharge volume, reduced lubrication, vaginal dryness, and a rise in vaginal pH above 5.0. The altered microbiome creates susceptibility to urinary tract infections and recurrent bacterial vaginosis. Topical estrogen therapy effectively restores vaginal epithelial health, microbiome composition, and normal discharge characteristics.

Pregnancy

Pregnancy produces a dramatic increase in vaginal discharge volume — a normal phenomenon called leukorrhea of pregnancy. Elevated estrogen and progesterone together increase vaginal transudation, accelerate cervical mucus production, and stimulate epithelial cell shedding, producing copious clear-to-white, mild-smelling discharge throughout pregnancy. Progesterone also increases susceptibility to vulvovaginal candidiasis, making yeast infections substantially more common in pregnancy. Any discharge that becomes malodorous, purulent, or blood-stained during pregnancy warrants prompt evaluation.

Abnormal Discharge: Evidence-Based Differential Diagnosis

A Framework for Assessment

Clinicians and women themselves assess abnormal discharge by evaluating four cardinal features: colour, consistency, odour, and associated symptoms (itch, irritation, pelvic pain, dyspareunia). No single feature is pathognomonic, and the pattern of features in combination guides diagnosis.

A critical principle of vaginal discharge assessment is that clinical syndromic diagnosis — based on symptoms and examination alone — has poor diagnostic accuracy. Studies consistently show that experienced clinicians correctly identify the cause of abnormal discharge on examination alone in fewer than 50% of cases. Laboratory testing (microscopy, pH measurement, culture, and molecular assays) is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment, particularly given the clinical and microbiological overlap between bacterial vaginosis, trichomoniasis, and mixed infections.

Bacterial Vaginosis

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in women of reproductive age worldwide, responsible for approximately 40–50% of vaginal complaints presenting to primary care.

Pathophysiology of BV

BV represents a polymicrobial dysbiosis characterised by the near-complete replacement of the Lactobacillus-dominant community with a diverse overgrowth of anaerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria. The key pathogenic species include Gardnerella vaginalis (which forms the primary biofilm scaffolding on the vaginal epithelium), Prevotella spp., Mobiluncus spp., Mycoplasma hominis, and Ureaplasma spp. The degradation of vaginal mucus proteins by bacterial sialidase and prolidase enzymes, and the production of polyamines (putrescine, cadaverine, trimethylamine) by anaerobes, generates the characteristic "fishy" amine odour — particularly noticeable after intercourse when semen (alkaline) volatilises the amines.

Diagnosis: Amsel Criteria and Nugent Scoring

Clinical diagnosis of BV uses the Amsel criteria — three of the following four must be present: homogeneous, thin, white-grey discharge; vaginal pH above 4.5; positive whiff (amine) test on addition of 10% potassium hydroxide; and clue cells (epithelial cells heavily coated with bacteria) comprising more than 20% of epithelial cells on wet mount microscopy.

The Nugent score provides a more objective laboratory standard, quantifying the relative proportions of Lactobacillus morphotypes (large gram-positive rods) and BV-associated morphotypes (small gram-variable rods, curved rods) on Gram-stained vaginal smears. A Nugent score of 0–3 is normal, 4–6 is intermediate, and 7–10 is BV.

BV affects approximately 29% of reproductive-age women in the United States and up to 50% of women in some sub-Saharan African populations. It is associated with a 2–3-fold increased risk of acquisition of HIV and other STIs, 2-fold increased risk of preterm birth, increased susceptibility to post-surgical pelvic infections, and higher rates of pelvic inflammatory disease. Standard treatment with metronidazole (oral 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, or 0.75% vaginal gel for 5 days) achieves 70–80% cure rates at 4 weeks, but recurrence within 12 months affects up to 50% of women.

Vulvovaginal Candidiasis

Vulvovaginal candidiasis (VVC), commonly called a yeast infection, is the second most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge and affects approximately 75% of women at least once in their lifetime.

Pathophysiology and Predisposing Factors

Candida albicans is responsible for 85–90% of VVC cases, with C. glabrata and other non-albicans species accounting for the remainder. Candida is a normal commensal of the lower genital tract in 20–30% of women and causes disease only when the balance shifts to permit excessive hyphal growth. Predisposing factors include antibiotic use (most commonly implicated), pregnancy (elevated progesterone impairs cell-mediated immunity), uncontrolled diabetes mellitus (elevated glucose facilitates fungal growth), immunosuppression, and — according to recent research — specific genetic variants in pattern recognition receptors involved in anti-fungal immunity.

Clinical Features and Diagnosis

VVC produces a characteristic thick, white, curd-like or "cottage cheese" discharge that adheres to vaginal walls and lacks the malodour of BV. The hallmark symptom is vulvovaginal itch — often intense — accompanied by burning, dyspareunia, and external dysuria. Erythema, oedema, and fissuring of the vulva and vaginal introitus are common examination findings. Vaginal pH in VVC is typically normal (3.8–4.5), which is an important distinguishing feature from BV and trichomoniasis. Diagnosis is confirmed by microscopy (pseudohyphae and budding yeast cells on wet mount with 10% KOH) or culture.

Recurrent VVC (defined as four or more episodes per year) affects approximately 5–8% of reproductive-age women and represents a significant quality-of-life burden. Recent research using whole-genome sequencing has demonstrated that most recurrent infections represent reinfection from a persistent gastrointestinal reservoir rather than treatment failure or re-exposure from a sexual partner. This has therapeutic implications: suppressive maintenance therapy (weekly oral fluconazole for 6 months) reduces recurrence rates by approximately 90% during treatment but does not eradicate the underlying susceptibility.

Trichomoniasis

Trichomoniasis is the most common non-viral sexually transmitted infection worldwide, caused by the protozoan parasite Trichomonas vaginalis, and accounts for approximately 10–15% of vaginal discharge presentations.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Features

T. vaginalis is a flagellated protozoan that attaches to vaginal, cervical, and urethral epithelial cells, causing direct cytotoxicity and a pronounced inflammatory response. Classic discharge is profuse, yellow-green, frothy, and malodorous, with vaginal pH typically above 5.0. The pathognomonic "strawberry cervix" — petechial haemorrhages on the cervix — is visible in only 2–5% of infected women but is highly specific when present. Importantly, up to 70% of women and 85% of men with trichomoniasis are asymptomatic, making routine screening important in high-prevalence populations.

The WHO estimates 156 million new cases of trichomoniasis annually worldwide, with the highest burden in sub-Saharan Africa and among women over 40 in high-income countries — a demographic pattern distinct from most STIs. Trichomoniasis is associated with a 2-fold increase in HIV acquisition and transmission risk, adverse pregnancy outcomes including preterm birth and low birthweight, and increased susceptibility to cervical cancer. First-line treatment is metronidazole 2g single oral dose (cure rate 90–95%) or 500 mg twice daily for 7 days. Partner treatment is essential due to high re-exposure rates.

Sexually Transmitted Cervicitis: Chlamydia and Gonorrhoea

Cervical infection with Chlamydia trachomatis or Neisseria gonorrhoeae produces endocervicitis, which can manifest as abnormal vaginal discharge — though both infections are frequently asymptomatic.

Distinguishing Features and Risks of Under-Diagnosis

When symptomatic, chlamydial and gonococcal cervicitis produces a mucopurulent discharge — yellow-green, purulent material arising from the cervical os rather than the vaginal walls, often with contact bleeding on cervical examination. Vaginal discharge in this context is the result of infected cervical secretion mixing with vaginal fluid. Chlamydia is the most common bacterial STI in most high-income countries, affecting approximately 4–5% of sexually active women aged 15–25 years, with the majority having no symptoms. Untreated, ascending infection causes pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) in up to 15% of cases, with PID being the leading preventable cause of tubal infertility and ectopic pregnancy.

Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT) on vaginal swabs — which women can self-collect with high accuracy — is now the gold standard for diagnosis of both chlamydia and gonorrhoea, with sensitivity exceeding 95% compared to 60–85% for culture. Annual chlamydia screening is recommended for all sexually active women under 25 in most national guidelines, regardless of symptoms. A vaginal discharge presentation should always prompt consideration of chlamydia and gonorrhoea testing, even when the discharge characteristics suggest a non-STI cause.

Aerobic Vaginitis

Aerobic vaginitis (AV) is a distinct vaginal syndrome recognised more clearly since the early 2000s and characterised by an inflammatory overgrowth of aerobic enteric bacteria rather than the anaerobic dysbiosis of BV.

Pathophysiology and Clinical Features

AV is caused by aerobic organisms — most commonly Group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Enterococcus faecalis — in the absence of the usual Lactobacillus flora. Unlike BV, AV generates a significant local inflammatory response, with elevated vaginal IL-6, IL-8, and leukocyte elastase. Discharge in AV is typically yellow-green, homogeneous, and associated with vaginal erythema and soreness — an inflammatory picture absent in BV. Diagnosis requires a combined assessment of discharge colour, leukocyte count, parabasal cell proportion, and lactobacillary grade on wet mount microscopy (the AV score). AV is associated with preterm birth and ascending neonatal infection in pregnancy.

Less Common but Important Causes

A number of less common conditions can present with abnormal discharge and should be considered when first-line diagnoses have been excluded or when standard treatments fail.

Desquamative Inflammatory Vaginitis, Foreign Bodies, and Fistulae

Desquamative inflammatory vaginitis (DIV) is a poorly understood chronic inflammatory condition characterised by purulent discharge, erythema, and significant parabasal cell shedding. It may represent a variant of severe AV or a distinct immunological syndrome. It is managed with vaginal clindamycin and/or topical corticosteroids.

Foreign body vaginitis produces a chronic, profuse, malodorous discharge — most commonly in prepubertal girls (retained toilet paper, small objects) and in adults (forgotten tampons, pessaries, or contraceptive devices). A characteristic extremely foul, sometimes blood-tinged discharge should always prompt consideration of a retained foreign body.

Vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistulae produce a discharge with distinctive characteristics: watery, continuous, and potentially faeculent — and are important to exclude in women with a history of obstetric trauma, pelvic surgery, radiation, or inflammatory bowel disease.

Diagnostic Tests: From Clinic to Molecular Laboratory

Point-of-Care Testing

Several simple tests available in primary care and sexual health clinic settings provide rapid, objective information about vaginal discharge that significantly improves diagnostic accuracy over clinical assessment alone.

pH Testing, Whiff Test, and Wet Microscopy

Vaginal pH measurement using narrow-range indicator paper (distinguishing between 4.0 and 6.0) is perhaps the single most useful point-of-care test. A pH above 4.5 effectively excludes VVC (pH normal in candidiasis) and is highly sensitive for BV, trichomoniasis, AV, and atrophic vaginitis. A pH below 4.5 in the context of discharge symptoms makes BV and trichomoniasis unlikely without other evidence.

Wet mount microscopy performed within 10 minutes of sample collection allows assessment of clue cells (BV), pseudohyphae (VVC), motile trichomonads (trichomoniasis), parabasal cells (AV/atrophic change), and leukocyte density. In experienced hands, it provides a rapid, comprehensive assessment, though sensitivity for trichomoniasis (50–60%) is lower than NAAT.

Molecular and Genomic Diagnostics

Advances in molecular diagnostics have transformed the accuracy of vaginal discharge assessment in the past two decades.

NAAT Panels and Microbiome Profiling

Multiplex NAAT panels can simultaneously detect and differentiate BV-associated organisms, Candida species, T. vaginalis, C. trachomatis, and N. gonorrhoeae from a single vaginal swab with sensitivities exceeding 90% for most targets. Commercial panels (such as BD MAX Vaginal Panel) have demonstrated substantially higher accuracy than clinical diagnosis alone in large validation studies and are increasingly recommended in settings where microscopy is unavailable.

Research-grade 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and whole-metagenome sequencing now allow quantitative profiling of the entire vaginal microbiome community, identifying community state type and specific pathogenic consortia. While currently restricted to research settings, these tools are likely to enter clinical practice as costs fall.

Treatment Principles and Special Considerations

Avoiding Over-Treatment: The Importance of Accurate Diagnosis

A consistent finding across both clinical and community-based research is that women — and clinicians — frequently over-treat vaginal discharge based on symptoms alone, without laboratory confirmation.

Studies of women self-treating vulvovaginal symptoms with over-the-counter antifungal medications show that only 33–35% of women who self-diagnose and self-treat a yeast infection actually have VVC. The majority have BV, mixed infections, or non-infective causes, meaning antifungal treatment is both ineffective and potentially harmful in up to two-thirds of self-treatment episodes. Delayed accurate diagnosis of BV is associated with increased STI risk, ongoing symptoms, and unnecessary antibiotic courses.

Restoring the Vaginal Microbiome After Treatment

A growing body of research addresses strategies to restore a healthy Lactobacillus-dominant microbiome after antibiotic treatment for BV, reducing the high recurrence rates that currently limit treatment success.

Probiotics and Vaginal Microbiome Restoration

Oral and vaginal probiotics containing L. crispatus or L. rhamnosus strains have been investigated as adjuncts to standard antibiotic therapy for BV. A 2020 randomised controlled trial demonstrated that vaginal L. crispatus (LACTIN-V) applied vaginally after metronidazole treatment reduced BV recurrence by approximately 30% over 12 weeks compared to placebo — the first high-quality trial to demonstrate a significant microbiome restoration benefit. Oral probiotics have a weaker and less consistent evidence base, partly because Lactobacillus species must migrate from the gastrointestinal tract to the vagina, a route with uncertain efficiency.

The Role of the Sexual Partner

The extent to which male partners contribute to BV recurrence via sexual transmission is an area of active research with important clinical implications.

BV as a Sexually Associated (if Not Strictly Sexually Transmitted) Condition

BV-associated bacteria, including Gardnerella vaginalis and Prevotella species, are consistently detected in the penile microbiome of male partners of BV-positive women, and sexual debut is associated with first acquisition of BV in studies of young women. However, randomised trials of partner treatment (with metronidazole) have not demonstrated significant reductions in BV recurrence in female index cases — suggesting that while sexual transmission contributes, it is not the sole or even dominant driver of recurrence. Concurrent female partner treatment in women who have sex with women (WSW) does significantly reduce recurrence, consistent with bidirectional transmission in this population.

Psychosocial Dimensions of Vaginal Discharge

Discharge Anxiety and Health-Seeking Behaviour

The psychosocial burden of vaginal discharge — both normal and abnormal — is substantial and frequently underappreciated in clinical encounters.

Normal Discharge and the "Problem" of Physiological Leukorrhea

Qualitative studies consistently document high levels of anxiety about normal vaginal discharge, with women frequently interpreting normal physiological variation as evidence of infection, uncleanliness, or pathology. This anxiety is amplified by inadequate education, cultural taboos around female genital discussion, and the commercial marketing of feminine hygiene products that pathologise normal bodily secretions. Women who present repeatedly for assessment of normal discharge warrant reassurance, education about the cervical cycle, and screening for health anxiety rather than repeated microbiological testing.

A 2019 population-based study of over 3,000 women in the United Kingdom found that 42% of reproductive-age women reported using internal vaginal washing products, despite national guidance advising against this practice. Women who douched were significantly more likely to report abnormal discharge (partly a consequence of the douching itself) and more likely to present to primary care with vaginal symptoms. Educational interventions addressing normal discharge physiology reduced douching rates and primary care presentations in follow-up studies.

Stigma and Delayed Presentation

The stigma associated with symptoms below the waist — and particularly with STI-related causes of discharge — creates significant barriers to timely healthcare-seeking, with clinical consequences.

Studies of chlamydia and BV diagnosis find median symptom-to-presentation intervals of 4–8 weeks, during which ascending infection and transmission to partners can occur. Interventions shown to reduce stigma-related delay include normalising conversations about vaginal health in primary care consultations, non-judgmental sexual history-taking, and the availability of self-sampling (home vaginal swab) pathways for STI testing — the latter having expanded substantially since the COVID-19 pandemic accelerated telehealth and postal testing infrastructure.

Practical Guidance: When to Seek Assessment

Signs That Warrant Clinical Assessment

Change from personal baseline: Any new change in colour, volume, consistency, or odour that differs noticeably from what is normal for that individual

Malodour: A fishy or unpleasant smell, especially if worse after intercourse — the most specific symptom of BV

Itch or irritation: Vulvovaginal itch, burning, or soreness — the cardinal symptoms of VVC but also present in AV and trichomoniasis

Colour changes: Yellow-green, grey, or frankly purulent discharge; brown or blood-tinged discharge outside of menstruation

Associated symptoms: Pelvic pain, deep dyspareunia, post-coital bleeding, or urinary symptoms alongside discharge changes

New sexual partner or STI risk: Any change in discharge following a new sexual partner or potential STI exposure

Pregnancy: Any new or changing discharge during pregnancy warrants assessment given the adverse obstetric consequences of untreated BV and STIs

Treatment failure: Discharge that persists or recurs after standard antibiotic or antifungal treatment — suggesting misdiagnosis, resistant organism, or a mixed infection

Emerging Research Directions

The Vaginal Microbiome and HIV Prevention

The relationship between vaginal microbiome composition and HIV acquisition risk is one of the most active areas in reproductive infectious disease research, with potential implications for HIV prevention strategies in high-incidence populations.

Microbiome, Mucosal Immunity, and Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis

Studies from South African cohorts have demonstrated that CST IV vaginal microbiome composition — characterised by Gardnerella and anaerobic dysbiosis — is associated with a 4-fold increased risk of HIV acquisition, partly through mucosal inflammation and partly through upregulation of HIV co-receptor expression on vaginal CD4+ T cells. Of additional clinical significance, tenofovir-based vaginal microbicide gel (an HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis formulation) had dramatically reduced efficacy in women with non-Lactobacillus-dominant microbiomes, apparently because anaerobic bacteria metabolised the drug before it could reach target cells. This finding has reshaped thinking about microbiome-drug interactions in HIV prevention.

Vaginal Discharge Proteomics and Biomarker Discovery

High-resolution proteomic and metabolomic profiling of vaginal fluid is identifying biomarker signatures that may enable non-invasive diagnosis of reproductive conditions beyond infection.

Endometriosis, Preterm Birth, and Cervical Cancer Biomarkers

Proteomic studies have identified distinct protein expression patterns in vaginal fluid from women with endometriosis, including elevated levels of inflammatory markers that differ from those in infection-related discharge. Cervicovaginal fluid metabolomics is being explored as a non-invasive screening tool for preterm birth risk, with altered short-chain fatty acid profiles showing promise as predictive biomarkers in prospective cohort studies. Methylation patterns in cervicovaginal cells collected via routine swabs have demonstrated accuracy approaching that of formal cervical biopsy for triage of HPV-positive women in cervical cancer screening programmes.

Microbiome-Based Therapeutics

The success of the LACTIN-V trial and accumulating evidence on the importance of L. crispatus dominance has catalysed significant investment in next-generation vaginal microbiome therapeutics.

Live Biotherapeutic Products and Engineered Probiotics

Live biotherapeutic products (LBPs) — regulatory-grade, defined-composition microbial preparations — are in Phase II and III trials for BV recurrence prevention, with several L. crispatus-based products entering clinical development since 2020. Engineered probiotic strains expressing heterologous bacteriocins or anti-biofilm enzymes targeting Gardnerella biofilm are in preclinical development. Vaginal microbiome transplantation — transfer of cervicovaginal secretion from a L. crispatus-dominant donor to a recipient with recurrent BV — has been reported in a small proof-of-concept study with promising early results.

Summary: Key Principles of Vaginal Discharge Science

Evidence-Based Takeaways

Normal vaginal discharge is not a sign of poor hygiene or ill health — it is an active, hormonally regulated secretion essential for vaginal protection and reproductive function.

The vaginal microbiome — dominated by Lactobacillus species — is the central regulator of vaginal health, and its disruption by douching, antibiotics, or hormonal changes is the common pathway to most infectious discharge conditions.

Discharge characteristics change predictably across the menstrual cycle, with periovulatory mucus being normally abundant, clear, slippery, and elastic — a pattern that many women mistake for pathology.

Clinical diagnosis of abnormal discharge without laboratory confirmation is inaccurate — pH testing, microscopy, and NAAT are essential for correct diagnosis and appropriate treatment selection.

Self-treatment without diagnosis leads to treatment of the wrong condition in the majority of cases, delaying appropriate care and contributing to antibiotic overuse.

Vaginal hygiene requires only external washing with water or gentle unscented soap — the internal vaginal environment is self-cleaning through its secretions and microbiome, and internal washing is actively harmful.

Vaginal discharge sits at the intersection of immunology, microbiology, endocrinology, and reproductive medicine — and the science surrounding it has advanced dramatically in the past two decades. From the community state type framework of the vaginal microbiome to molecular diagnostics that outperform clinical judgement, the tools to understand and manage vaginal discharge scientifically now exist. What remains needed is better translation of this science into public education and clinical practice, so that the many women experiencing anxiety about normal discharge receive reassurance, and those with pathological conditions receive timely, accurate diagnosis and treatment.

The vagina is among the most resilient and self-regulating environments in the human body. Given the right conditions — adequate estrogen, an intact Lactobacillus community, freedom from unnecessary chemical disruption — it maintains its own health with remarkable efficiency. Understanding this biology is perhaps the most empowering knowledge a woman can have about her own body.
```
