PCOS Is Now PMOS: The Landmark 2026 Name Change

Everything you need to know about polycystic ovary syndrome's new name — and why it matters for your health

On May 12, 2026, a global consensus published in The Lancet officially renamed polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) to polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS) — the result of over a decade of advocacy, 22,000 survey respondents, and collaboration across 56 leading medical and patient organisations worldwide. This is not merely a cosmetic change: it reflects a fundamental reconceptualisation of a condition that affects 1 in 8 women — over 170 million people globally — and has long been misunderstood, misdiagnosed, and undertreated. This comprehensive guide covers everything from the science of the condition itself to the reasons for the rename, what PMOS actually is, how it differs in understanding from the old PCOS framework, and what this means for diagnosis and treatment going forward.

Why the Name Had to Change: The Problem With "PCOS"

A Misnomer Decades in the Making

For decades, doctors and the general public have thought that the name "polycystic ovary syndrome" was scientifically inaccurate. The term implied that the defining feature was the presence of abnormal cysts on the ovaries — but this was never true. What ultrasounds actually detect are arrested follicles: small, fluid-filled sacs containing eggs that have failed to mature and release normally. These are not pathological cysts in a clinical sense.

What "Polycystic" Refers To

A polycystic ovary on ultrasound contains an abnormally large number of developing follicles — typically 20 or more per ovary — giving it the appearance of many small cysts. However, having multiple follicles is also seen in other conditions and even in healthy women with high ovarian reserve. The presence of polycystic ovary morphology (PCOM) on imaging is just one of three diagnostic criteria, and many women with PCOS/PMOS never show it at all. The "cysts" are arrested follicles, not the disease itself.

The consequences of this misnaming were serious and measurable. The old terminology contributed to:

Documented Harms of the PCOS Label

Delayed diagnosis: Up to 70% of affected individuals remained undiagnosed, in part because the name directed clinical attention toward ovarian imaging rather than metabolic and hormonal markers.

Fragmented care: Women were shuffled between gynaecologists, endocrinologists, and dermatologists with no single clinician owning the whole picture — because the name made it seem like a gynaecological disorder.

Stigma and confusion: Patients told they have "polycystic ovaries" but then told they don't actually have cysts experienced confusion, distress, and distrust in the healthcare system.

Curtailed research funding: Framing the condition as primarily a reproductive/gynaecological issue deprioritised metabolic, cardiovascular, and neurological research avenues.

Inadequate treatment: When the hormonal and metabolic underpinnings were overlooked, treatment defaulted to hormonal birth control to manage symptoms — without addressing the root drivers of the condition.

A survey of over 14,360 people with PCOS/PMOS and multidisciplinary health professionals from all world regions found strong support for a name change. The consensus process engaged 56 leading academic, clinical, and patient organisations, and involved iterative global surveys and structured workshops over more than a decade of advocacy.

The Historical Origins of the PCOS Label

To understand why the old name persisted for so long, it helps to trace how it was coined in the first place. The term "polycystic ovary syndrome" emerged from a 1935 paper by American gynaecologists Irving Stein and Michael Leventhal, who described seven women with enlarged, cyst-studded ovaries, irregular menstruation, and virilising features. Stein and Leventhal performed bilateral ovarian wedge resection — surgically removing a wedge of ovarian tissue — and observed that menstruation resumed in most patients, implying the ovary was the root of the problem.

The Stein-Leventhal Legacy

For decades, "Stein-Leventhal syndrome" was the dominant label, and its gynaecological framing shaped an entire generation of clinical thinking. When the condition was later renamed "polycystic ovary syndrome" to reflect its broader symptom profile, the name still anchored the condition in ovarian structure rather than hormonal and metabolic function. The NIH's first formal diagnostic criteria in 1990 required hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction — but the "polycystic" of the name continued to misdirect both clinicians and the public. Ultrasound technology, which became widely available through the 1980s, paradoxically reinforced the misnaming: every radiologist report describing "polycystic ovaries" on imaging further entrenched the false idea that cysts were the defining pathology.

By the 2000s, endocrinologists and reproductive specialists increasingly recognised that insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction were not peripheral features of PCOS but central to its biology. Yet the name — and the clinical culture it had created — proved extraordinarily resistant to change. Patients diagnosed in the 1990s passed down their understanding of the condition to daughters diagnosed in the 2010s; general practitioners trained in an era of gynaecological framing continued to refer patients to gynaecologists first; and pharmaceutical trials remained organised around menstrual and reproductive endpoints rather than metabolic and cardiovascular ones.

A 2022 qualitative study of 204 women with PCOS across Australia, the United Kingdom, and Canada found that 89% had not been informed by their diagnosing clinician that the condition involves insulin resistance. Over 60% believed their condition was primarily or exclusively a reproductive problem. Fewer than 1 in 4 had ever been screened for metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes risk, or cardiovascular risk markers at the time of diagnosis.

PMOS Is Not an Ovarian Disease — It Is a System-Wide Endocrine Disorder

This is the central insight that the name change from PCOS to PMOS was designed to encode. The word polyendocrine — many glands, many hormones — is not decorative. It is a clinical instruction: when a woman presents with the cluster of symptoms we call PMOS, the ovaries are rarely the originating problem. They are typically the loudest voice in a conversation that started somewhere else — in the thyroid gland, the adrenal glands, the pituitary, or in the inflammatory environment connecting all of them.

Under the old PCOS framework, the thyroid, adrenals, and prolactin appeared primarily in the differential diagnosis section — things to rule out before confirming PCOS. The PMOS framework turns this on its head. These are not alternative explanations to eliminate. They are co-drivers that must be identified, measured, and treated as part of the same management plan. In many women, treating one of these upstream systems — bringing TSH into optimal range, lowering cortisol, normalising prolactin — produces downstream improvements in cycles, ovulation, androgens, and fertility that no amount of ovary-focused intervention ever achieved.

The "poly" in polyendocrine matters. For a significant proportion of women with PMOS, the reproductive symptoms — irregular cycles, anovulation, hirsutism — are the end result of hormonal dysfunction originating in glands nowhere near the ovaries. Treating symptoms at the ovarian level while leaving the upstream driver unaddressed is like muting the fire alarm while the fire burns on.

The Thyroid Gland and PMOS: A Bidirectional Relationship

How Common Is Thyroid Dysfunction in PMOS?

The connection between thyroid dysfunction and PMOS is one of the most clinically important — and most consistently overlooked — relationships in women's endocrinology. The thyroid gland sits at the intersection of metabolism, immune function, ovarian activity, and insulin signalling. When it underperforms, even mildly, it amplifies virtually every feature of PMOS.

Prevalence data: A 2023–2024 prospective cross-sectional study of 161 women with PMOS and 75 age-matched controls found subclinical hypothyroidism (SCH) in 14.9% of the PMOS group versus 5.3% of controls. Even in women who did not meet the threshold for SCH, median TSH levels were significantly higher in PMOS (2.2 mIU/L vs 1.75 mIU/L). Separately, a 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Endocrinology found that Hashimoto's thyroiditis — the autoimmune form of hypothyroidism — occurs in 22.8% of women with PMOS compared to 5.7% of women without it: a nearly four-fold higher prevalence. Thyroid peroxidase (TPO) antibodies, the marker of autoimmune thyroid activity, are elevated in up to 27% of women with PMOS.

Why Are PMOS and Thyroid Dysfunction So Closely Linked?

The relationship is bidirectional and operates through at least four distinct mechanisms:

Shared inflammatory biology: Both PMOS and Hashimoto's thyroiditis are fundamentally inflammatory conditions. The chronic low-grade systemic inflammation that characterises PMOS — elevated TNF-α, IL-6, and CRP — is the same inflammatory milieu that promotes autoimmune activation of the kind that destroys thyroid tissue in Hashimoto's. Each condition feeds the other's inflammatory state. Women with both PMOS and Hashimoto's show significantly worse insulin resistance and metabolic markers than women with either condition alone.

Anovulation removes progesterone's immune brake: In healthy cycling women, progesterone produced after ovulation exerts anti-inflammatory, immune-regulating effects that counterbalance oestrogen's immune-stimulating activity. In PMOS, chronic anovulation means progesterone is near zero for months or years at a time. Without this brake, oestrogen overstimulates the immune system — raising the risk of autoimmune conditions including Hashimoto's thyroiditis.

Thyroid hormones regulate insulin sensitivity directly: Thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) regulate glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue. Even subclinical hypothyroidism — TSH elevated but T4 still technically normal — impairs insulin-mediated glucose disposal. This worsens the insulin resistance that is already a core feature of PMOS, creating a compounding metabolic burden. Thyroid dysfunction also raises LDL cholesterol and triglycerides and lowers HDL, stacking on top of PMOS's already atherogenic lipid profile.

TSH directly stimulates ovarian tissue: TSH receptors have been detected in ovarian tissue, including in granulosa and luteal cells. Elevated TSH appears to directly stimulate ovarian androgen production and impair normal follicle development — meaning that even a modestly elevated TSH can worsen the ovarian dysfunction of PMOS through a pathway entirely separate from the insulin and LH mechanisms already in play.

What "Normal" TSH Actually Means in PMOS — and Why It May Not Be Enough

One of the most practically important and underappreciated clinical points in PMOS management concerns the definition of a "normal" TSH. Standard laboratory reference ranges typically flag TSH as abnormal only above 4.0–5.0 mIU/L. But growing evidence — and the 2021 European Thyroid Association guidelines — suggest that for women with PMOS who want to conceive, this threshold is far too permissive.

The TSH Target Debate in PMOS and Fertility

The 2021 European Thyroid Association guidelines on thyroid disorders before and during assisted reproductive technology (ART) recommend levothyroxine for women with autoimmune thyroid disease (Hashimoto's) and TSH above 2.5 mIU/L — even if their TSH falls within the conventional "normal" range. This is because even TSH levels between 2.5 and 4.0 mIU/L are associated with measurably worse reproductive outcomes in women with underlying thyroid autoimmunity: higher miscarriage rates, lower implantation rates in IVF, and impaired embryo development. For women with PMOS who have elevated TPO antibodies, a TSH target of 1.0–2.5 mIU/L is increasingly recommended before conception attempts — a target many women with PMOS are not achieving without intervention, even those who have never been flagged as hypothyroid.

Levothyroxine and fertility outcomes: A prospective randomised trial in Fertility and Sterility studied 64 infertile women with subclinical hypothyroidism undergoing IVF/ICSI. The levothyroxine group (50μg from the first day of ovarian stimulation) achieved significantly higher clinical pregnancy rates and better embryological parameters than untreated controls. More broadly, a narrative review in Frontiers in Endocrinology (2023) concluded that women with both PMOS and subclinical hypothyroidism or autoimmune thyroiditis "represent a phenotype at higher risk of reproductive dysfunction" and a priority population for levothyroxine intervention — even though this is not yet formally incorporated into PCOS/PMOS treatment guidelines in most countries.

Real-World Impact: When Treating the Thyroid Changes Everything

The clinical literature, and the lived experience of many women with PMOS, contains numerous accounts of the following pattern: a woman has years of irregular cycles, failed ovulation induction, and unresolved hirsutism or weight resistance — all attributed to PCOS, with ovary-focused treatments cycling through without success. Then TPO antibodies or a borderline TSH is finally measured. Levothyroxine is started, TSH is brought to 1.5–2.0 mIU/L, and within 3–6 months: cycles regularise. Ovulation resumes. Insulin resistance improves. In some women, pregnancy follows spontaneously for the first time. This is not anecdote — it is the mechanistic consequence of removing a second major driver of insulin resistance, androgen excess, and anovulation that was operating silently beneath the PCOS diagnosis the entire time. The PMOS framework, by foregrounding thyroid assessment as a mandatory part of the initial workup rather than an optional ruling-out step, is designed to catch these women far earlier.

If you have PMOS, thyroid testing should include not just TSH but also free T4, free T3, and TPO antibodies. A TSH that falls within the conventional "normal" range may still be suboptimal for your reproductive and metabolic health. If you have elevated TPO antibodies and are trying to conceive, discuss with your endocrinologist whether a TSH target below 2.5 mIU/L is appropriate for you — even if you have never been formally diagnosed with hypothyroidism.

Hashimoto's, PMOS, and the Autoimmune-Inflammatory Amplification Loop

Hashimoto's thyroiditis is the most common autoimmune disease in women, and its co-occurrence with PMOS is not coincidental. The two conditions share a common inflammatory architecture — and when present together, they compound each other's impact on insulin resistance, fertility, and cardiovascular risk in ways that are clinically distinct from either condition alone.

The Compounding Effect of PMOS + Hashimoto's

Women with both PMOS and Hashimoto's thyroiditis show higher HOMA-IR scores, worse lipid profiles, more pronounced menstrual irregularity, and greater difficulty conceiving than women with PMOS alone — even when thyroid hormone levels are kept within normal range with treatment. This suggests that the autoimmune inflammation of Hashimoto's adds a layer of metabolic and reproductive harm beyond what thyroid hormone levels alone can account for. Elevated anti-TPO and anti-thyroglobulin antibodies are themselves associated with implantation failure and early pregnancy loss, independent of TSH levels. Managing the inflammatory burden of Hashimoto's — through selenium supplementation (which has RCT evidence for reducing TPO antibody titres), vitamin D optimisation, anti-inflammatory diet, and in some cases low-dose naltrexone — is increasingly viewed as part of comprehensive PMOS care for women with the dual diagnosis.

The Adrenal PMOS Phenotype

In the conventional PCOS framework, elevated androgens were assumed to come primarily from the ovaries — driven by excess LH stimulation. This is true for many women. But in approximately 20–30% of women with PMOS, a significant portion of the androgen excess originates not from the ovaries but from the adrenal glands — specifically from elevated production of dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate (DHEA-S). This is the adrenal PMOS phenotype, and it has distinct drivers, a distinct symptom pattern, and — critically — a distinct treatment approach.

How Adrenal Androgens Work in PMOS

The adrenal cortex produces androgens — primarily DHEA and DHEA-S — from a region called the zona reticularis, in response to ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) signalling from the pituitary. In women with adrenal PMOS, the zona reticularis is hyperresponsive to ACTH — meaning that even with normal or near-normal ACTH levels, the adrenals produce disproportionately large quantities of DHEA-S. This is not HPA axis dysfunction in the conventional sense; ACTH levels in women with PMOS are generally comparable to controls. The problem is at the adrenal level itself: a sensitised response that overproduces androgens in reply to normal hormonal signals.

DHEA-S itself is a weak androgen, but it serves as a substrate for conversion into more potent androgens — androstenedione, testosterone, and dihydrotestosterone (DHT, the most potent androgen) — in peripheral tissues including skin, hair follicles, and adipose tissue. This peripheral conversion means that a woman can have elevated DHEA-S, relatively normal serum testosterone, and still experience significant hirsutism, acne, and scalp hair loss — because the active androgen load is happening in the tissues, not in the bloodstream where standard tests measure it. Elevated DHEA-S levels are substantially heritable: studies suggest up to 67% of DHEA-S levels in women with PMOS are genetically determined, with genetic variants in the SULT2A1 enzyme gene playing a confirmed role.

Premature adrenarche as an early marker: Premature adrenarche — the early onset of adrenal androgen production in childhood, presenting as early pubic and axillary hair development before age 8 — increases the risk of developing PMOS by approximately 50%. Girls with premature adrenarche have elevated DHEA-S from childhood, and many go on to develop the full PMOS phenotype at puberty. This represents one of the earliest identifiable markers of adrenal PMOS risk — and one that is routinely missed in paediatric care because the connection to later adult hormone disorders is not widely recognised.

How Chronic Stress Drives Adrenal Androgen Excess

The relationship between psychological and physiological stress and PMOS is not metaphorical. It operates through a precise, measurable biological mechanism — and in women with adrenal PMOS, stress management is not a wellness adjunct; it is the primary intervention target.

The HPA Axis–PMOS Connection

When the body perceives stress — physical, psychological, or metabolic — the hypothalamus releases corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH), which signals the pituitary to release ACTH, which tells the adrenal glands to produce cortisol and androgens. In women with adrenal PMOS, this signal hits an already hyperresponsive adrenal cortex. The result: chronic, low-level stress — the kind produced by work pressure, poor sleep, disordered eating, over-exercise, blood sugar dysregulation, or chronic illness — produces chronically elevated adrenal androgen output. DHEA-S rises. Testosterone rises through peripheral conversion. Acne flares. Hair thins. Cycles destabilise. And the elevated cortisol independently worsens insulin resistance — feeding back into the core metabolic dysfunction of PMOS.

This is why so many women with adrenal PMOS describe a pattern that is exquisitely stress-sensitive: symptom flares during high-pressure life periods, relative improvement during holidays and rest, worsening during illness or burnout. The symptom is real. The mechanism is biological. And it points directly to an intervention strategy that the old PCOS framework had no room for: treating the adrenal driver means treating the stress response.

Insulin Resistance Amplifies Adrenal Androgen Production

Even where the adrenal glands are the primary androgen source, insulin resistance amplifies their output. Hyperinsulinaemia — the compensatory high insulin produced in response to insulin resistance — directly sensitises the adrenal cortex to ACTH, increasing DHEA-S and androstenedione production independently of the stress axis. This is why adrenal and ovarian PMOS phenotypes so frequently overlap: the metabolic driver (insulin resistance) stimulates both ovarian and adrenal androgen production simultaneously. In the majority of women with adrenal PMOS, treating insulin resistance — with metformin, inositols, dietary modification, and exercise — also reduces DHEA-S levels, typically by 15–30% within 3–6 months. This is one of the strongest arguments for metabolic-first treatment sequencing in all PMOS phenotypes, not just in those with classic ovarian androgen excess.

Diagnosing and Treating Adrenal PMOS

What to Test and What to Look For

DHEA-S measurement is the primary test for adrenal androgen excess. Levels above 350–400 μg/dL in adult women (with age-specific reference ranges) suggest meaningful adrenal contribution to the androgen picture. DHEA-S does not fluctuate with the menstrual cycle, making it a more stable and reliable marker than testosterone for serial monitoring. Morning cortisol and a 24-hour urinary free cortisol can identify HPA axis dysregulation. A DUTCH (Dried Urine Test for Comprehensive Hormones) test maps not just cortisol and DHEA-S but their downstream metabolites, providing a more complete picture of where androgens are being produced and how they are being converted peripherally — this level of detail can be particularly useful for women with adrenal PMOS who have normal serum testosterone but significant androgen symptoms.

Ruling out non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (NCAH) is essential before concluding that elevated DHEA-S reflects adrenal PMOS. NCAH — a genetic disorder causing impaired adrenal steroidogenesis — can produce a clinical picture virtually identical to adrenal PMOS, and is diagnosed by measuring 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), ideally in the early morning. NCAH has a specific treatment (low-dose glucocorticoids) that is distinct from adrenal PMOS management.

Treatment Approaches for Adrenal PMOS

Stress regulation as primary treatment: For adrenal PMOS, reducing chronic HPA axis activation is the most direct intervention. Evidence-backed approaches include restorative yoga (not hot or high-intensity), daily mindfulness or breathwork, sleep prioritisation, and addressing blood sugar dysregulation — which itself acts as a physiological stressor that chronically activates the HPA axis. These are not soft recommendations: they target the specific biological mechanism driving adrenal androgen overproduction.

Insulin sensitisation: Metformin reduces adrenal androgen levels — specifically DHEA-S — within 3–6 months in women with insulin-driven adrenal excess, by reducing the hyperinsulinaemia that amplifies adrenal androgen output. Myo-inositol produces comparable effects. GLP-1 agonists also reduce adrenal androgen excess as part of their broader insulin-sensitising action.

Anti-androgenic OCPs or spironolactone: In women with symptomatic adrenal androgen excess (significant hirsutism, acne, or alopecia), anti-androgenic OCPs or spironolactone can be layered on top of metabolic management to block peripheral androgen action while the root drivers are addressed.

Low-dose glucocorticoids (specific cases only): In women confirmed to have non-classical CAH, low-dose glucocorticoids (prednisolone or dexamethasone) suppress ACTH-driven adrenal androgen overproduction. In uncomplicated adrenal PMOS without CAH, glucocorticoids are not routinely recommended given their metabolic side effect profile — they worsen insulin resistance and weight — which directly counteracts the metabolic goals of PMOS management.

Prolactin and PMOS

What Prolactin Does — and What Happens When It's Too High

Prolactin is produced by the pituitary gland and is best known for driving milk production after childbirth. But it has a much broader role in reproductive function — and when it is chronically elevated outside of pregnancy and breastfeeding (a condition called hyperprolactinaemia), it becomes one of the most potent suppressors of ovulation known in human endocrinology.

How Hyperprolactinaemia Causes PMOS-Like Symptoms

Excess prolactin acts directly on the hypothalamus to suppress GnRH pulsatility — the very same neuroendocrine disruption that is a core feature of PMOS. When GnRH pulses are suppressed, LH and FSH fall, follicles cannot develop or ovulate, and the cycle stops. Prolactin also suppresses kisspeptin neurons — the upstream regulators of GnRH — creating a double inhibitory effect on the entire reproductive axis. The resulting clinical picture is anovulation, oligomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea, and in some women, galactorrhoea (spontaneous breast milk production outside of pregnancy). This presentation overlaps substantially with PMOS, which is why hyperprolactinaemia must always be excluded before a PMOS diagnosis is confirmed.

Importantly, prolactin elevation is not always obvious or dramatic. Mildly elevated prolactin — in the range of 25–50 ng/mL (normal upper limit typically 20–25 ng/mL) — can produce significant reproductive disruption without galactorrhoea and without the headaches or visual disturbances associated with larger prolactinomas. This mild-moderate range is easily missed if prolactin is not routinely measured or if a borderline result is dismissed as a stress artefact without follow-up.

Prevalence in PMOS: Mild to moderate hyperprolactinaemia is found in approximately 10–40% of women with PMOS across different study populations. Some of this elevation is secondary — driven by the same disrupted hypothalamic signalling that characterises PMOS — rather than caused by a prolactinoma. But even secondary prolactin elevation compounds anovulation and makes the reproductive picture significantly harder to treat if left unaddressed. Stress, hypothyroidism, certain medications (antipsychotics, some antiemetics, some antidepressants), and heavy exercise can all raise prolactin — all of which are factors more common in women with PMOS than in the general population.

Causes of Elevated Prolactin in Women With PMOS

The Most Important Causes to Rule In

Prolactinoma: A benign pituitary tumour producing excess prolactin. Microprolactinomas (under 10mm) are the most common, frequently missed on routine imaging, and yet produce prolactin levels sufficient to cause complete anovulation and cycle loss. They are substantially more common in women than men and peak in the reproductive years. Pituitary MRI is required for diagnosis.

Hypothyroidism (including subclinical): This is the critical connection between the thyroid and prolactin sections of this discussion. Hypothyroidism — including subclinical hypothyroidism — raises TRH (thyrotropin-releasing hormone), which in turn stimulates prolactin secretion from the pituitary. A woman with undiagnosed or undertreated hypothyroidism may therefore have elevated prolactin as a secondary consequence, causing anovulation that appears to be PMOS but is actually driven by thyroid-pituitary crosstalk. Treating the hypothyroidism with levothyroxine normalises TRH, which then normalises prolactin, which then restores GnRH pulsatility, which then restores ovulation. This is the mechanistic chain behind the clinical observation that women with previously unexplained anovulation sometimes conceive after thyroid treatment alone — a pattern that was anecdotally known for decades but is now mechanistically understood.

Medications: Antipsychotic medications (which block dopamine receptors), metoclopramide, domperidone, and some selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) raise prolactin. Women with PMOS who are also being treated for anxiety or depression — a common scenario given the elevated mental health burden of PMOS — may have medication-driven prolactin elevation compounding their anovulation.

Physiological stress and HPA activation: Chronic physical and psychological stress elevates prolactin through CRH-mediated pathways. This creates a direct link between the adrenal stress response and prolactin-driven anovulation — yet another reason why stress management is a therapeutic intervention in PMOS, not merely a wellness recommendation.

Treating Hyperprolactinaemia in PMOS: What the Outcomes Show

Dopamine agonists — the case for treatment: When prolactin elevation is identified as a driver of anovulation in a woman with PMOS, the treatment response is among the most dramatic in reproductive endocrinology. Cabergoline (taken once or twice weekly) achieves normalisation of prolactin levels in approximately 95% of patients, menstrual cycle regulation in 94% of those with normalised prolactin, and pregnancy in approximately 60–80% of women whose only cause of infertility was prolactin-driven anovulation. In the words of one large systematic review: "in cases where chronic anovulation due to hyperprolactinaemia is the only cause of infertility, a 60–80% pregnancy rate can be achieved" with dopamine agonist treatment — without letrozole, without IVF, without any ovary-directed intervention at all.

Cabergoline vs Bromocriptine: What to Know

Bromocriptine was the original dopamine agonist for hyperprolactinaemia, available since the 1970s, and remains in use. Cabergoline — available since the 1990s — has largely superseded it due to superior prolactin normalisation rates, better tolerability (fewer gastrointestinal side effects), and once or twice weekly dosing versus daily bromocriptine. Cabergoline normalises prolactin in 95% of microprolactinoma patients versus 80–90% for bromocriptine. Both are considered safe during early pregnancy, but the standard recommendation is to discontinue both agents once pregnancy is confirmed, as they cross the placenta. Bromocriptine has a more extensive safety record in pregnancy due to its longer clinical history. For women with PMOS and concurrent hyperprolactinaemia who are trying to conceive, the choice between agents should be made in collaboration with an endocrinologist familiar with both the PMOS phenotype and prolactinoma management.

Prolactin should be measured in every woman receiving a PMOS workup — not as a differential to rule out, but as an active co-driver to identify. Mild prolactin elevation (25–50 ng/mL) in a woman with anovulation and PMOS features warrants investigation and often treatment, not dismissal. If you have PMOS and have not had your prolactin measured, ask for it. If it is elevated, ask for a repeat fasting measurement (prolactin rises with food, stress, and recent breast stimulation) and, if confirmed elevated, ask about pituitary MRI and specialist referral.

Putting It Together: The PMOS Endocrine Web

What emerges from understanding thyroid, adrenal, and prolactin contributions to PMOS is not a list of separate problems sitting alongside the condition. It is a picture of a single, interconnected endocrine system in which disruption at any one node reverberates through all the others. This is precisely what the name polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome was chosen to convey.

How the Glands Feed Each Other in PMOS

Hypothyroidism → insulin resistance: Reduced thyroid hormone impairs glucose uptake in muscle and adipose tissue, worsening the core insulin resistance of PMOS. This raises insulin levels, which drive further ovarian and adrenal androgen production.

Hypothyroidism → elevated prolactin: Raised TRH (the thyroid-stimulating signal from the hypothalamus) also stimulates prolactin release from the pituitary. Undiagnosed subclinical hypothyroidism can therefore produce secondary hyperprolactinaemia — and secondary anovulation — through a pathway completely invisible to ovary-focused PCOS management.

Adrenal hyperresponsiveness → more androgens → worse insulin resistance: DHEA-S and adrenal androgens convert peripherally to testosterone and DHT, amplifying the androgen excess that already exists from ovarian sources. Excess androgens in turn worsen insulin resistance through circadian clock gene mechanisms — creating a self-reinforcing loop between the adrenal gland and the metabolic dysfunction of PMOS.

Chronic stress → HPA activation → cortisol → insulin resistance: Elevated cortisol — whether from psychological stress, poor sleep, blood sugar dysregulation, or inflammatory illness — directly induces insulin resistance and raises blood glucose, requiring more insulin, amplifying hyperinsulinaemia, and driving further androgen production from both ovarian and adrenal sources.

Anovulation → no progesterone → autoimmune activation → worsens Hashimoto's: The absence of progesterone from anovulatory cycles removes the immune brake, allowing oestrogen to overstimulate the immune system. This promotes autoimmune activity — including the thyroid antibody production of Hashimoto's — which feeds back to worsen thyroid function, insulin resistance, and reproductive outcomes.

Prolactin elevation → GnRH suppression → worsened anovulation: Whether from a microprolactinoma, hypothyroidism-driven TRH elevation, medication, or stress, excess prolactin suppresses GnRH pulsatility — the neuroendocrine disruption already present in PMOS — compounding its severity and making ovulation harder to restore with standard interventions.

This interlocking endocrine picture explains a clinical pattern familiar to many women with PMOS: the experience of having ovary-focused treatments — OCPs, letrozole, clomiphene — fail repeatedly, followed by the eventual discovery of an elevated TSH, a borderline prolactin, or elevated DHEA-S that had been present the entire time. When the upstream driver is finally treated, the downstream symptoms — cycles, ovulation, androgen levels, fertility — often respond in ways that years of ovary-directed treatment did not achieve. The PMOS framework exists, in part, to ensure that these upstream drivers are found at the beginning of the diagnostic journey — not after years of frustration.

What a Complete PMOS Endocrine Workup Should Include

Under the PMOS framework, the following represents a minimum comprehensive endocrine assessment at the time of diagnosis — not a menu of optional extras, but a baseline that reflects the polyendocrine nature of the condition.

The Complete PMOS Endocrine Panel

Ovarian / reproductive: LH, FSH, LH:FSH ratio, total testosterone, free androgen index, oestradiol (day 2–5), AMH, progesterone (day 21 if cycling), pelvic ultrasound or transvaginal ultrasound for follicle count and ovarian volume.

Thyroid: TSH, free T4, free T3, anti-TPO antibodies, anti-thyroglobulin antibodies. If antibodies elevated or TSH above 2.5 mIU/L in a woman trying to conceive: consider thyroid ultrasound and endocrinology referral.

Adrenal: DHEA-S (for adrenal androgen contribution), morning cortisol (if clinical features of cortisol excess or HPA dysregulation), 17-hydroxyprogesterone (to exclude non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia — ideally measured in the early follicular phase before 9am).

Prolactin: Fasting, mid-morning prolactin measurement (repeat if elevated to exclude stress artefact). If consistently elevated: pituitary MRI and endocrinology referral.

Metabolic: Fasting glucose, fasting insulin (HOMA-IR calculation), full lipid panel, liver enzymes (ALT, AST, GGT), HbA1c, vitamin D (25-OH), full blood count (iron, ferritin — elevated in some PMOS phenotypes; low in others with heavy or irregular bleeding).

Cardiovascular baseline: Blood pressure, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, BMI (with awareness of its limitations in PMOS).

Mental health: GAD-7 (anxiety), PHQ-9 (depression) at every initial assessment and annual review.

PMOS is not a diagnosis of exclusion — something left over after everything else has been ruled out. It is a positive diagnosis of a multisystem endocrine and metabolic condition. The thyroid, adrenal glands, and pituitary are not alternative explanations for your symptoms. They are part of the same condition — and testing them is not ruling out PMOS; it is completing the PMOS picture.

Ethnic and Geographic Variation in PMOS Expression

PMOS does not present uniformly across populations. Understanding how the condition manifests differently across ethnic groups is clinically important because it affects diagnostic thresholds, risk stratification, and treatment priorities.

PMOS Across Populations: Key Differences

South Asian women (particularly those from India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka) tend to develop PMOS at lower BMI thresholds, with more severe insulin resistance relative to body weight. Metabolic complications — including type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease — occur earlier and at lower levels of obesity. Hirsutism may be scored differently due to baseline differences in body hair distribution across ethnic groups, making the Ferriman-Gallwey scale less reliable when applied without population-adjusted cutoffs.

East Asian women commonly present with lean PMOS: insulin resistance is present but obesity is less frequently a feature. This population has historically been underdiagnosed because the clinical stereotype of PMOS as a condition of overweight women does not apply. AMH levels and ultrasound morphology remain valid markers, but metabolic assessment should not be deferred on the assumption that a low BMI confers metabolic protection.

Hispanic and Latina women show higher rates of severe hyperandrogenism and greater insulin resistance compared to non-Hispanic White women at equivalent BMI, with correspondingly elevated risks of gestational diabetes and type 2 diabetes progression.

Women of African descent are significantly underrepresented in PMOS research, creating evidence gaps. Available data suggest that hyperandrogenism may be underdiagnosed in this group due to inadequate assessment tools and clinical bias. Cardiovascular risk appears elevated, but longitudinal data are limited.

Indigenous Australian and New Zealand Māori women face compounding disadvantages: higher baseline metabolic risk, healthcare access barriers, and cultural factors that affect health-seeking behaviour, all of which increase the gap between PMOS prevalence and diagnosis rates.

The 2026 consensus explicitly identifies equity in PMOS care as a priority, noting that women from minority ethnic backgrounds, lower socioeconomic groups, and rural/remote communities consistently receive later diagnoses, less comprehensive workups, and less access to evidence-based treatments. Culturally adapted PMOS resources, community health worker training, and revised anthropometric thresholds for different ethnic groups are among the recommended implementation priorities.

The Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Ovarian Axis in PMOS

To understand PMOS, it helps to understand how the normal hormonal system works — and how it goes wrong.

Normal HPO Axis Function

The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) in pulses, which signals the pituitary to release follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH). FSH drives follicle development in the ovaries; a rising LH surge at mid-cycle triggers ovulation. The resulting corpus luteum produces progesterone, completing the cycle. In PMOS, this elegant feedback system is disrupted at multiple levels simultaneously.

Neuroendocrine Disruption in PMOS

One of the hallmark features of PMOS is increased GnRH pulsatility — the hypothalamus fires GnRH signals too frequently. This drives disproportionately high LH secretion relative to FSH. Elevated LH stimulates ovarian theca cells to produce excess androgens (testosterone and androstenedione), while relatively low FSH means follicles cannot complete development or convert androgens to oestrogen efficiently. The result: follicles stall, androgens accumulate, and ovulation fails.

Hyperandrogenism: The Defining Endocrine Feature

Elevated androgens — primarily testosterone, free androgen index, and androstenedione — are present in the majority of PMOS cases and drive many of its most visible symptoms. Androgens come from two sources in PMOS: the ovaries (stimulated by excess LH) and, in some cases, the adrenal glands.

Clinical Manifestations of Hyperandrogenism

Hirsutism: Excess coarse hair growth on the face, chest, abdomen, and back, affecting 60–80% of women with PMOS. Scored clinically using the Ferriman-Gallwey scale.

Acne vulgaris: Androgen-driven sebaceous gland overstimulation, often persistent into adulthood and resistant to topical treatments alone.

Androgenic alopecia: Diffuse scalp hair thinning or male-pattern hair loss, affecting 20–30% of women with PMOS.

Metabolic amplification: Excess androgens worsen insulin resistance, creating a vicious cycle.

Insulin Resistance: The Metabolic Engine

Insulin resistance is arguably the most important feature of PMOS from a long-term health perspective — and the one most overlooked under the old PCOS framework. When cells stop responding normally to insulin, the pancreas compensates by producing more, leading to compensatory hyperinsulinaemia.

Insulin resistance and compensatory hyperinsulinaemia are present in 85% of all individuals with PMOS — including 75% of lean women (BMI ≤25 kg/m²). This means insulin resistance in PMOS is not simply a consequence of excess body weight; it is a core, intrinsic feature of the condition at the cellular level. This finding, confirmed in The Lancet 2026 consensus, is one of the strongest scientific arguments for the name change.

How Insulin Resistance Drives PMOS

Hyperinsulinaemia directly stimulates ovarian theca cells to produce more androgens — independently of LH stimulation. It also decreases hepatic production of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), meaning more testosterone circulates in its biologically active (free) form. Additionally, insulin resistance in the ovaries themselves impairs normal follicle development, contributing to anovulation. This metabolic-endocrine feedback loop is self-reinforcing: more androgens drive more insulin resistance, which drives more androgens.

Adipose Tissue as an Active Endocrine Organ in PMOS

Fat tissue is not merely a passive energy store — it is an active endocrine organ that produces hormones (adipokines), inflammatory cytokines, and sex steroids. In PMOS, the function of adipose tissue is dysregulated in ways that amplify the hormonal and metabolic disruptions of the condition.

Adipokine Dysregulation in PMOS

Leptin: Produced by fat cells, leptin signals satiety to the hypothalamus and plays a role in regulating GnRH pulsatility. In PMOS, leptin resistance is common — particularly in women with excess visceral adiposity — meaning the hypothalamus fails to appropriately downregulate appetite signalling and GnRH pulse frequency. This may directly contribute to the neuroendocrine disruption central to PMOS.

Adiponectin: This anti-inflammatory, insulin-sensitising adipokine is consistently reduced in women with PMOS, particularly those with central obesity. Low adiponectin correlates with higher HOMA-IR, lower HDL cholesterol, and higher triglycerides. Adiponectin reduction appears to be partly driven by excess androgens — which suppress its production — creating another self-reinforcing loop.

Resistin and IL-6: Pro-inflammatory adipokines including resistin and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are elevated in PMOS, contributing to a state of chronic low-grade systemic inflammation. This inflammation is now recognised as a driver of both insulin resistance and ovarian dysfunction, not merely a consequence of obesity.

Visceral vs subcutaneous fat: Women with PMOS tend to store a disproportionate amount of fat viscerally (around the abdominal organs) rather than subcutaneously, even at the same total body weight as unaffected women. Visceral adiposity is metabolically more harmful — it is more insulin resistant, more pro-inflammatory, and more strongly associated with cardiovascular risk — which explains why metabolic complications in PMOS often exceed what body weight alone would predict.

A 2023 imaging study using DEXA (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) found that lean women with PMOS had significantly higher visceral-to-subcutaneous fat ratios compared to BMI-matched controls — meaning that even without visible excess weight, women with PMOS carry a disproportionate metabolic burden in their fat distribution pattern. This finding reinforces why waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio should be routinely measured in PMOS assessment regardless of overall BMI.

Chronic Inflammation: The Shared Mechanism

One of the most important biological insights of the past decade has been the recognition that PMOS is fundamentally an inflammatory condition — not merely hormonally or metabolically disordered. Chronic low-grade systemic inflammation ties together the disparate features of the syndrome: insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, anovulation, cardiovascular risk, and mental health burden.

Inflammatory Biomarkers in PMOS

Women with PMOS consistently show elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-18, tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1) compared to matched controls. These elevations are present even in lean women and even in women without overt metabolic syndrome, suggesting that inflammation is intrinsic to PMOS biology rather than a downstream consequence of obesity. CRP levels independently predict cardiovascular outcomes and insulin resistance progression in PMOS cohorts. Anti-inflammatory dietary interventions — including Mediterranean-pattern diets, omega-3 supplementation, and high-polyphenol food choices — have been shown to reduce inflammatory biomarkers in PMOS and improve metabolic outcomes, independent of weight change.

The Four PMOS Phenotypes

PMOS is not one condition but a spectrum of related presentations. The Rotterdam Criteria — still the gold standard for diagnosis — identify four phenotypes based on combinations of three core features:

Phenotype A (Classic)

Ovulatory dysfunction + hyperandrogenism + polycystic ovaries. Most common and most severe metabolic profile. Highest insulin resistance and testosterone levels.

Phenotype B (NIH)

Ovulatory dysfunction + hyperandrogenism. No PCOM required. Originally the only phenotype recognised by the NIH in 1990. Strong androgen and metabolic features.

Phenotype C (Ovulatory)

Hyperandrogenism + polycystic ovaries. Normal ovulatory cycles. Sometimes called "mild PMOS." Metabolic risk lower but still present.

Phenotype D (Non-androgenic)

Ovulatory dysfunction + polycystic ovaries. No clinical hyperandrogenism. Most controversial phenotype — lowest metabolic burden, some dispute inclusion.

A cross-sectional study of 111 women with PMOS found an overall metabolic syndrome prevalence of 33.6%. Importantly, there was no statistically significant difference in metabolic syndrome prevalence across the four phenotypes, suggesting that metabolic risk is present across the entire spectrum of the condition — not just in those with the most severe hormonal presentation. Studies also show Phenotype A has the highest HOMA-IR scores and the highest LH/FSH ratios, while lean PMOS women show elevated LH/FSH but lower triglyceride and blood pressure levels compared to obese PMOS women.

Diagnosis: How PMOS Is Identified

The Rotterdam Criteria (2003, Still Current)

The Rotterdam Criteria remain the most widely used diagnostic framework for PMOS. An adult is diagnosed when at least two of the following three criteria are present, after excluding other causes of hyperandrogenism and ovulatory dysfunction:

Rotterdam Diagnostic Criteria for PMOS

1. Ovulatory dysfunction: Irregular or absent menstrual cycles (oligomenorrhoea: fewer than 9 cycles per year; amenorrhoea: no cycle for 90+ days). May also include subtle anovulation in women with apparently regular cycles.

2. Clinical and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism: Hirsutism (Ferriman-Gallwey score ≥6), acne, or alopecia; OR elevated biochemical androgen markers (total testosterone, free androgen index, DHEAS).

3. Polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM): ≥20 follicles per ovary on ultrasound, OR elevated anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels (now increasingly preferred over ultrasound, especially in younger women). AMH reflects total follicle pool and correlates strongly with PMOS diagnosis.

A critical update in recent guidelines: anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) is increasingly used as a surrogate for PCOM on ultrasound, particularly in adolescents and women where transvaginal ultrasound is not appropriate. AMH is produced by small antral follicles and is substantially elevated in PMOS — typically 2–3 times the levels seen in unaffected women. However, age-specific AMH reference ranges for PMOS diagnosis are still being refined and represent a key open research question flagged in the 2026 consensus.

The Diagnostic Odyssey: Why Women Wait Years for Answers

The average 2–3 year delay from symptom onset to PMOS diagnosis is not simply a clinical failure — it is a systemic one, reflecting how medical education, clinical incentives, and even diagnostic technologies have been organised around the old PCOS framework.

Barriers to Timely PMOS Diagnosis

Symptom normalisation: Acne, irregular periods, and hair changes are extremely common in adolescence, and many clinicians — and parents — reassure young women that these are normal pubertal variations rather than investigating their underlying cause. This is appropriate caution in some cases, but structured monitoring for PMOS over a 2-year post-menarchal window is rarely implemented.

BMI bias: PMOS in lean women is frequently missed or dismissed. Clinicians trained to associate PMOS with obesity may reassure a woman with a "healthy" BMI that she could not have the condition, even when she presents with irregular cycles, elevated testosterone, and other classic markers. The 2026 consensus emphasises that lean PMOS carries equivalent endocrine burden and requires the same comprehensive metabolic workup as PMOS in overweight women.

Reproductive focus: Many women with PMOS first receive a thorough diagnostic workup only when they present with infertility — meaning that the decade or more of metabolic harm accumulating between adolescent onset and attempted conception may go entirely unaddressed. The PMOS framework aims to shift diagnosis to the onset of symptoms rather than waiting for a fertility crisis.

Laboratory variation: Testosterone assays vary significantly in sensitivity and reference ranges between laboratories, particularly for measurements in the low-to-normal female range. Borderline results may be reported as "normal" by one laboratory but "elevated" by another, leading to diagnostic confusion. The 2026 consensus calls for standardised testosterone assay criteria and the development of harmonised reference ranges across laboratory systems.

Healthcare access: Comprehensive PMOS diagnosis requires access to blood tests, pelvic ultrasound (or AMH testing), and ideally a clinician with endocrine expertise. In low- and middle-income countries, and in under-resourced regions of high-income countries, these resources may not be available, leading to syndromic diagnosis based on clinical features alone — which is valid under the Rotterdam criteria but less precise.

Differential Diagnoses to Exclude

Before diagnosing PMOS, clinicians must rule out other conditions that can cause similar features. These include congenital adrenal hyperplasia (particularly non-classical 21-hydroxylase deficiency), Cushing's syndrome, androgen-secreting tumours, thyroid disorders, and hyperprolactinaemia.

Required Exclusion Testing

Standard diagnostic workup includes: thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), prolactin, 17-hydroxyprogesterone (to exclude congenital adrenal hyperplasia), total and free testosterone, DHEAS, fasting insulin and glucose (HOMA-IR), lipid panel, and in some cases a morning cortisol. Pelvic ultrasound and/or AMH measurement completes the assessment.

Diagnosis in Adolescents

Diagnosing PMOS in teenagers is challenging because irregular cycles and mild acne are common in the first 2–3 years after menarche as part of normal pubertal development. Current guidelines recommend caution in adolescents, generally requiring all three Rotterdam criteria to be present — or a 2-year observation period — before confirming the diagnosis.

Long-Term Health Risks Associated With PMOS

Metabolic and Cardiovascular Disease

The metabolic sequelae of PMOS represent the most significant long-term health burden. Insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism together create an environment of chronic low-grade inflammation, dyslipidaemia, and vascular dysfunction.

Women with PMOS face a 2–3 times higher risk of developing type 2 diabetes compared to unaffected women, with impaired glucose tolerance present in 30–40% of those with PMOS even in their twenties and thirties. Risk is independent of obesity, though excess weight significantly amplifies it. Cardiovascular disease risk is elevated 1.5–2 fold, with higher rates of hypertension, adverse lipid profiles (elevated LDL and triglycerides, low HDL), and subclinical atherosclerosis detected even in lean young women with PMOS.

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD)

Formerly called non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, MASLD affects 30–40% of women with PMOS — roughly double the rate in the general female population at equivalent BMI. Insulin resistance drives hepatic de novo lipogenesis and impairs beta-oxidation, leading to fat accumulation in liver cells. Elevated liver enzymes and abnormal liver function on ultrasound should be screened at diagnosis and monitored regularly.

Cardiovascular Risk: The Full Picture

Cardiovascular disease is one of the leading causes of death in women globally, and PMOS substantially accelerates the trajectory toward cardiovascular events. The mechanisms are multiple and intersecting, making cardiovascular risk reduction a central rather than peripheral goal of PMOS management.

Cardiovascular Mechanisms in PMOS

Endothelial dysfunction: The inner lining of blood vessels — the endothelium — shows impaired function in women with PMOS even in early adulthood. Studies using flow-mediated dilatation (FMD) — a sensitive measure of vascular health — consistently show reduced FMD in PMOS compared to matched controls, indicating early vascular stiffening and reduced nitric oxide bioavailability. This endothelial impairment is present in lean women with PMOS and correlates with hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance independently of BMI.

Dyslipidaemia: The lipid profile characteristic of PMOS is atherogenic: elevated LDL cholesterol (particularly small dense LDL particles, which are more penetrating of arterial walls), elevated triglycerides, and low HDL. This pattern closely resembles the lipid profile of type 2 diabetes and is driven by both insulin resistance (which impairs lipoprotein lipase activity) and excess androgens (which alter hepatic lipid metabolism).

Hypertension: Blood pressure is elevated in women with PMOS compared to matched controls from the second decade of life onwards. The mechanisms include hyperinsulinaemia-driven renal sodium retention, sympathetic nervous system activation, and vascular stiffening from endothelial dysfunction.

Subclinical atherosclerosis: Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores and carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) — established markers of subclinical atherosclerosis — are elevated in premenopausal women with PMOS compared to controls. These findings indicate that the arterial damage associated with cardiovascular disease begins earlier in PMOS than in the general female population, and that standard risk calculators (which weight heavily for age) may significantly underestimate cardiovascular risk in young women with PMOS.

The 2026 PMOS consensus recommends that cardiovascular risk assessment should begin at the time of PMOS diagnosis — not decades later. This includes a baseline lipid panel, blood pressure, waist circumference, fasting glucose and insulin, and liver enzymes. Women with additional cardiovascular risk factors (family history, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, or impaired fasting glucose) should receive intensified monitoring and early intervention, including consideration of statin therapy in appropriate cases.

Endometrial Cancer Risk

Women with PMOS have approximately a 3-fold higher risk of endometrial cancer compared to the general population. The mechanism involves chronic unopposed oestrogen exposure: when ovulation fails to occur, the corpus luteum does not form, so progesterone is not produced. Without progesterone's counterbalancing effect, oestrogen continuously stimulates endometrial proliferation, eventually leading to hyperplasia and potential malignant transformation.

Long-term anovulation is a recognised risk factor for endometrial cancer. Women with PMOS who are not menstruating regularly should be advised to induce a withdrawal bleed at least every 3–4 months using progestogens, to shed the endometrial lining. This is an important and often missed clinical management point.

Ovarian and Breast Cancer: Current Evidence

Beyond endometrial cancer, research has examined whether PMOS confers elevated risk for ovarian and breast cancers. The evidence here is more nuanced and, in some areas, still evolving.

Cancer Risk in PMOS: What the Evidence Shows

Ovarian cancer: Several large cohort studies and meta-analyses have found a modest elevation in ovarian cancer risk in PMOS — approximately 1.4–2 fold. The mechanism is not fully established but may involve chronic gonadotropin stimulation (elevated LH) of ovarian epithelium, hormonal promotion of borderline ovarian tumours, or shared genetic risk factors affecting both PMOS and ovarian malignancy. The absolute risk remains low given the relatively low baseline incidence of ovarian cancer, but the association is clinically relevant for surveillance decisions.

Breast cancer: The relationship between PMOS and breast cancer is complex and possibly bidirectional. Oestrogen exposure promotes certain breast cancer subtypes, while androgens may have protective effects on some breast tissues but promote others. Population-level studies have shown inconsistent results — some suggesting modestly elevated risk, others showing no significant difference or even reduced rates of oestrogen-receptor positive breast cancer in PMOS. The 2026 consensus characterises breast cancer risk as "uncertain but not clearly elevated" and does not recommend PMOS-specific breast cancer screening beyond standard population guidelines.

Colorectal cancer: Emerging evidence suggests that the metabolic syndrome features of PMOS — insulin resistance, hyperinsulinaemia, visceral adiposity — may increase colorectal cancer risk, consistent with established links between these metabolic features and colon cancer in the general population. This is an active area of investigation.

Sleep Disorders and PMOS

Sleep disturbances — particularly obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) — are substantially more prevalent in women with PMOS than in matched controls, even after adjusting for BMI. This association is biologically plausible and clinically important, yet remains underappreciated and under-screened.

Women with PMOS have an estimated 5–30 times higher prevalence of obstructive sleep apnoea compared to weight-matched controls. Androgens are thought to promote OSA through effects on upper airway musculature and central respiratory drive. Insulin resistance and visceral adiposity further increase risk. OSA in PMOS creates a compounding metabolic burden: sleep fragmentation worsens insulin resistance, promotes cortisol dysregulation, increases appetite-driving hormones (ghrelin), and reduces leptin sensitivity — all of which worsen the metabolic profile of PMOS. Women with PMOS who report excessive daytime sleepiness, snoring, or observed apnoeas should be referred for sleep study (polysomnography or home sleep testing).

Restless Legs Syndrome and PMOS

Restless legs syndrome (RLS) — the urge to move the legs, especially at night, often associated with uncomfortable sensations — is also elevated in PMOS, with prevalence approximately 3 times higher than in unaffected women. The mechanism is unclear but may involve dopaminergic dysregulation (which is also implicated in the neuroendocrine features of PMOS), iron metabolism disturbances (which are common in PMOS due to menstrual irregularity and iron deficiency), and the inflammatory milieu of PMOS. RLS significantly impairs sleep quality and daytime functioning and warrants assessment in women with PMOS presenting with sleep complaints.

Neurological and Cognitive Effects

PMOS has effects beyond the classic reproductive-metabolic-dermatological triad. Emerging evidence points to neurological and cognitive dimensions of the condition that may be relevant across the life span.

Brain Health in PMOS

Cognitive function: Several studies have found subtle differences in cognitive performance in women with PMOS compared to controls, particularly in areas of working memory, processing speed, and executive function. These differences are modest and do not constitute clinically significant cognitive impairment, but they suggest that the brain is affected by PMOS biology — potentially through the effects of insulin resistance (which impairs cerebral glucose metabolism), androgens (which affect neuroreceptor sensitivity), and chronic inflammation (which promotes neuroinflammation).

Dementia risk: Population-level studies have found that women with PMOS history have modestly elevated risks of dementia and cognitive decline in later life, consistent with the known links between insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and Alzheimer's disease. This association is particularly notable given that women already have higher baseline dementia risk than men; PMOS may amplify this sex-specific vulnerability.

Headache and migraine: Migraine prevalence is elevated in PMOS, with hormonal fluctuations — particularly the androgen excess and oestrogen-progesterone imbalances characteristic of anovulatory cycles — thought to act as migraine triggers. Women with PMOS who experience frequent migraines may benefit from hormonal stabilisation as part of their management plan.

Mental Health: A Core Feature, Not a Side Effect

The 2026 PMOS consensus officially recognises psychological symptoms as core features of the syndrome, not secondary complaints. This is a major shift from the PCOS framework, where mental health was largely an afterthought.

Women with PMOS experience rates of anxiety approximately 5 times higher and depression approximately 3 times higher than matched unaffected women. Eating disorder prevalence is elevated 3–4 fold. Body image distress, sexual dysfunction, and reduced quality of life are consistently reported across multiple countries and cultures. Importantly, mental health burden in PMOS appears to be driven not just by visible symptoms (hirsutism, acne, weight) but also by the hormonal biology itself — particularly androgenic effects on mood regulation and the psychological toll of diagnostic delays.

The Psychological Burden: A Deeper Examination

Understanding the mental health impact of PMOS requires appreciating both the biological and psychosocial dimensions of the condition. The distress is not simply a reaction to difficult symptoms — it appears to be, at least in part, a direct neurobiological consequence of PMOS itself.

Biological and Psychosocial Drivers of Mental Health Burden in PMOS

Androgen effects on brain chemistry: Androgens have direct neuroactive effects, influencing serotonin, dopamine, and GABA neurotransmission. Chronic androgen excess may dysregulate mood-stabilising neurotransmitter systems, contributing to anxiety and depressive symptoms independently of the psychosocial burden of living with the condition. This biological contribution to mental health is an active area of neuropsychiatric research.

HPA axis dysregulation: The hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis — the body's stress response system — shows altered reactivity in PMOS. Cortisol responses to psychological stress are modified, and adrenal androgen production (DHEAS) is elevated in a subset of women with PMOS. HPA dysregulation may contribute to anxiety, sleep disruption, fatigue, and mood instability.

Diagnostic delay and medical invalidation: Years of normalised symptoms, dismissed concerns, and misdiagnoses create a form of iatrogenic psychological harm. Many women with PMOS describe being told their symptoms are "stress," "hormonal," or "just how their body is" before receiving a diagnosis. This invalidation erodes confidence in the healthcare system and in one's own bodily perception.

Visible symptoms and body image: Hirsutism, acne, and weight changes directly impact self-image, romantic relationships, and social functioning. Stigma around "feminine" body ideals creates particular distress around facial hair and scalp hair loss. Importantly, the severity of psychological distress does not correlate neatly with the severity of physical symptoms — women with mild hirsutism may experience profound distress, while those with severe hirsutism report relative acceptance. This underscores the importance of individual, person-centred assessment rather than relying on clinical severity scores alone.

Eating disorders and disordered eating: Women with PMOS are at markedly elevated risk for binge eating disorder, bulimia nervosa, and restrictive eating patterns. Weight stigma in clinical consultations — including clinicians recommending weight loss without providing tools or acknowledging psychological barriers — is associated with increased eating disorder risk. Dietary counselling for PMOS should always incorporate a weight-neutral, anti-stigma approach alongside evidence-based nutritional guidance.

The PMOS framework mandates that a validated anxiety and depression screening tool (such as the GAD-7 and PHQ-9) be administered at every initial PMOS assessment and at every annual review. Women screening positive should be offered a clear referral pathway to psychological support — not simply reassured and dismissed. Clinical psychologists and health psychologists with experience in chronic conditions and body image distress are particularly valuable members of the PMOS multidisciplinary team.

Reproductive Health and Fertility

PMOS is one of the most common causes of female infertility, primarily through anovulation. However, the picture is nuanced: many women with PMOS do conceive spontaneously, and the condition does not mean infertility is inevitable.

Fertility Outcomes in PMOS

With appropriate management, the majority of women with PMOS who wish to conceive can do so. First-line fertility treatment is ovulation induction with letrozole (an aromatase inhibitor), which achieves live birth rates of 27–30% per cycle. Clomiphene citrate, the previous standard, is now second-line due to inferior outcomes. In vitro fertilisation (IVF) is effective in PMOS but carries elevated risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) due to the high follicle reserve. Agonist trigger protocols and freeze-all strategies substantially reduce OHSS risk.

PMOS is responsible for approximately 70–80% of anovulatory infertility cases. Miscarriage rates are elevated at approximately 30–50% in untreated PMOS (compared to 15–20% in the general population), linked to elevated LH levels and insulin resistance impairing endometrial receptivity. Metformin pre-treatment has been shown in some studies to reduce early miscarriage rates by improving the uterine environment.

Treatment of PMOS: What the New Framework Means in Practice

The Paradigm Shift: Metabolic First

The single most important practical consequence of the PCOS-to-PMOS rename is a change in the order of operations for treatment. The old framework typically led with hormonal birth control (OCP) to manage symptoms. This can be valid — but it doesn't address insulin resistance. The new PMOS framework mandates that metabolic drivers are addressed first, with hormonal symptom management layered on top.

PMOS Treatment Hierarchy (New Framework)

1. Lifestyle modification as foundation: Diet optimisation (low-glycaemic index, adequate protein, anti-inflammatory), structured exercise (combined aerobic + resistance training), sleep quality, stress reduction. Even a 5–10% weight loss in overweight women dramatically improves insulin sensitivity, androgen levels, and ovulatory frequency.

2. Insulin-sensitising therapy: Metformin is the most evidence-based pharmacological intervention, reducing hepatic glucose production and improving peripheral insulin sensitivity. Myo-inositol (a natural insulin sensitiser) has comparable efficacy to metformin in some studies with fewer gastrointestinal side effects. Combined myo-inositol/D-chiro-inositol supplementation is increasingly used.

3. Weight management (when indicated): GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide) are emerging as foundational treatments for women with weight-driven PMOS, not last resorts. Early data is promising for androgen reduction, menstrual regularity, and metabolic markers.

4. Hormonal support layered on top: Combined oral contraceptives, androgen receptor blockers (spironolactone, flutamide), or anti-androgens as part of a broader plan — not as the primary or sole intervention.

5. Mental health integration: Proactive screening for anxiety and depression at diagnosis; referral pathways integrated into standard care rather than treated separately.

Nutrition in PMOS: Beyond "Eat Less"

Nutritional management of PMOS has evolved considerably beyond generic advice to reduce calories and lose weight. Evidence now supports specific dietary patterns that target the underlying metabolic and inflammatory biology of the condition.

Evidence-Based Dietary Approaches for PMOS

Low glycaemic index (GI) diet: Foods that cause slow, gradual rises in blood glucose reduce postprandial insulin surges — the key mechanism by which diet impacts PMOS biology. A low-GI diet does not require calorie restriction and is sustainable long-term. Practical foods include legumes, whole grains, most vegetables, most fruits (particularly berries), and lean proteins. High-GI foods to reduce include white bread, white rice, processed cereals, sweet beverages, and most ultra-processed snack foods.

Mediterranean diet pattern: The Mediterranean diet — rich in vegetables, legumes, whole grains, olive oil, fish, and moderate amounts of poultry, dairy, and red wine — consistently improves insulin sensitivity, reduces inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6), and improves lipid profiles in PMOS. Multiple randomised trials confirm that Mediterranean-pattern eating is among the most effective dietary interventions for metabolic health in PMOS, including in lean women.

Protein adequacy: Adequate dietary protein (targeting 1.2–1.6g per kg body weight per day) supports muscle mass maintenance, improves satiety, and reduces postprandial glucose excursions. High-protein breakfasts in particular appear to reduce evening appetite and binge eating tendencies — which are relevant given the elevated eating disorder risk in PMOS.

Anti-inflammatory foods: Beyond macronutrient composition, specific food categories have anti-inflammatory properties relevant to PMOS. These include omega-3-rich fatty fish (salmon, sardines, mackerel), extra virgin olive oil, curcumin (turmeric), green tea, dark leafy vegetables, and polyphenol-rich berries. Regular inclusion of these foods reduces CRP and improves endothelial function independently of weight change.

What does NOT have strong evidence: Keto and very-low-carbohydrate diets show short-term improvements in insulin resistance in PMOS but poor long-term adherence and potential downsides for gut microbiome diversity. Dairy restriction is commonly recommended online but lacks robust clinical evidence in PMOS. Intermittent fasting shows promise in small trials but needs larger PMOS-specific studies before routine recommendation.

A systematic review of 10 randomised controlled trials comparing dietary patterns in PMOS found that low-GI diets reduced fasting insulin by an average of 12% and testosterone by 8% compared to standard dietary advice — without requiring caloric restriction. Mediterranean diet interventions additionally reduced CRP by 28% and improved endothelial function scores. These effects are clinically meaningful and represent achievable, sustainable improvements that can be achieved before any pharmacological intervention is initiated.

Exercise in PMOS: The Right Prescription

Exercise is a powerful, evidence-based treatment for PMOS — but the type, intensity, and combination of exercise matters. The outdated advice to simply "exercise more" misses the specificity that makes exercise genuinely therapeutic for PMOS biology.

Exercise Prescription for PMOS

Resistance training: Progressive resistance training (weights, resistance bands, bodyweight exercises) is arguably the most metabolically important form of exercise for PMOS. It builds skeletal muscle, which is the body's primary site of insulin-mediated glucose uptake. Increasing muscle mass improves whole-body insulin sensitivity profoundly and durably. Guidelines recommend at least 2–3 resistance sessions per week, targeting all major muscle groups, with progressive loading over time.

Aerobic exercise: Moderate-intensity continuous aerobic exercise (brisk walking, cycling, swimming) is effective for cardiovascular fitness, caloric balance, and mood. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) appears particularly effective for insulin sensitivity improvements per unit time, with several PMOS-specific trials showing HIIT reduces HOMA-IR and testosterone levels more effectively than moderate continuous exercise in equivalent time periods.

Combined approach: The evidence is clearest for a combination of resistance training plus aerobic exercise. Randomised trials in PMOS consistently show greater improvements in insulin resistance, androgen levels, body composition, and menstrual regularity with combined training versus either modality alone. A practical minimum is 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity plus 2 resistance sessions per week.

Avoiding overtraining: Exercise at extremes can worsen HPA axis dysregulation and impair recovery in women with PMOS. Excessive exercise without adequate nutrition — common in women attempting rapid weight loss — elevates cortisol, worsens insulin resistance, and can paradoxically suppress ovulation further. Sustainable, progressive exercise with adequate nutritional support is more beneficial than intense exercise regimes.

Metformin and Insulin Sensitisers

Metformin, a biguanide originally developed for type 2 diabetes, has been used in PMOS/PCOS management for over three decades. It works primarily by reducing hepatic glucose production and improving insulin receptor sensitivity in muscle and adipose tissue.

Evidence Base for Metformin in PMOS

Meta-analyses consistently show that metformin improves menstrual regularity, reduces testosterone levels, lowers fasting insulin, and modestly improves ovulation rates in women with PMOS. Gastrointestinal side effects (nausea, diarrhoea) affect approximately 20–30% of users; extended-release formulations significantly reduce this. Metformin is safe in long-term use and is one of few PMOS treatments with evidence for cardiovascular risk reduction.

GLP-1 Receptor Agonists: The New Frontier

GLP-1 (glucagon-like peptide-1) receptor agonists — including liraglutide (Victoza/Saxenda), semaglutide (Ozempic/Wegovy), and exenatide — are now attracting substantial interest as PMOS treatments, particularly for metabolically-driven presentations.

GLP-1 agonists work by mimicking the natural GLP-1 hormone, stimulating insulin secretion in a glucose-dependent manner, suppressing glucagon, slowing gastric emptying, and acting centrally to reduce appetite. In women with PMOS, early trial data shows GLP-1 agonists reduce body weight by 8–15% on average, lower testosterone levels significantly, improve menstrual regularity, reduce HOMA-IR by 30–45%, and improve lipid profiles. A completed Phase 4 randomised clinical trial (NCT03151005) comparing metformin-GLP-1 agonist combination versus metformin-OCP in overweight PMOS demonstrated superior cardiovascular risk marker improvement in the GLP-1 group.
GLP-1 agonists are not yet formally approved for PMOS treatment — their current FDA and TGA indications are type 2 diabetes and chronic weight management. However, given that insulin resistance is confirmed as a core feature of PMOS in 85% of cases, the 2026 consensus explicitly frames these medications as "increasingly viewed as foundational treatments rather than last resorts" for women with significantly weight-driven or metabolically active PMOS. Insurance coverage remains inconsistent for this indication.

Emerging and Investigational Treatments

Beyond the current evidence base, several promising investigational treatments are in active clinical development for PMOS. These represent the therapeutic pipeline that the new PMOS framework — with its broader metabolic framing — is expected to accelerate.

Therapeutic Pipeline for PMOS

GIP/GLP-1 dual agonists (tirzepatide): Tirzepatide (Mounjaro/Zepbound), a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and GLP-1 receptor agonist, achieves greater weight reduction and insulin sensitisation than GLP-1 agonists alone. Phase 2 trials in PMOS are ongoing; early results suggest superior improvements in testosterone, HOMA-IR, and body composition compared to semaglutide monotherapy. If confirmed in Phase 3 trials, tirzepatide may become a preferred pharmacological agent for metabolically severe PMOS.

Kisspeptin analogues: Kisspeptin is a neuropeptide that directly regulates GnRH pulse secretion — the upstream neuroendocrine dysfunction central to PMOS. Kisspeptin analogues and kisspeptin receptor modulators are in early clinical development as targeted treatments to normalise GnRH pulsatility in PMOS, which could address the hormonal dysregulation at its source rather than managing downstream consequences. This represents one of the most mechanistically precise therapeutic approaches ever investigated for the condition.

Neurokinin B (NKB) antagonists: Hyperactivation of neurokinin B signalling in the hypothalamus drives the increased GnRH pulsatility of PMOS. NKB receptor antagonists (NK3R antagonists) — originally developed for menopausal hot flushes — have shown in Phase 2 trials that they reduce LH pulse frequency, lower testosterone levels, and restore ovulatory cycles in PMOS. This is an entirely new class of mechanism-targeted treatment for PMOS.

SGLT-2 inhibitors: Drugs originally developed for type 2 diabetes that reduce renal glucose reabsorption (e.g., empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) have shown metabolic benefits in PMOS in small trials — reducing insulin levels, body weight, and androgen levels. Their role in PMOS management is not yet established, but their dual cardiorenal protective properties make them candidates for women with PMOS who also have elevated cardiovascular or renal risk.

Gut microbiome modulation: Growing evidence implicates gut microbiome dysbiosis in PMOS pathophysiology. Women with PMOS show altered gut microbial compositions — reduced microbial diversity, lower abundance of butyrate-producing species, and higher levels of lipopolysaccharide-producing bacteria — compared to unaffected women. Interventions targeting the gut microbiome, including probiotics, prebiotics, dietary fibre, and faecal microbiota transplant (FMT), are under active investigation.

Combined Oral Contraceptives (OCP) in PMOS

OCPs remain valuable tools in PMOS management — but with a reframed role. They do not address insulin resistance and in some formulations may slightly worsen it. Their primary utility is in managing androgen-driven symptoms and regulating cycles when metabolic treatment alone is insufficient.

How OCPs Help in PMOS

Oestrogen in combined pills increases hepatic SHBG production, reducing free (active) testosterone levels by 40–60%. Progestins suppress LH, reducing ovarian androgen production. Anti-androgenic progestins — particularly cyproterone acetate (Brenda-35/Diane-35 in Australia), drospirenone (Yasmin/Yaz), and chlormadinone acetate — provide additional direct blockade of androgen receptors. These formulations are particularly useful for acne, hirsutism, and cycle regulation. However, per the PMOS framework, OCPs are most effective when started after metabolic stabilisation rather than as the first and only intervention.

Anti-Androgens

For women not seeking contraception or those with inadequate OCP response, anti-androgens can be used directly. Spironolactone (50–200mg/day) blocks androgen receptors and inhibits ovarian androgen production; it is among the most effective treatments for hirsutism and acne in PMOS. Flutamide and bicalutamide are alternatives with strong evidence but are less commonly used due to hepatotoxicity concerns (flutamide) or limited PMOS-specific data (bicalutamide).

Inositols: The Emerging Supplement

Myo-inositol and D-chiro-inositol are naturally occurring members of the vitamin B complex that play critical roles in insulin signalling. Women with PMOS have a demonstrated defect in inositol phosphoglycan metabolism and often exhibit relative D-chiro-inositol deficiency in ovarian tissue.

Multiple randomised controlled trials and meta-analyses have shown that myo-inositol supplementation (typically 4g/day) achieves insulin sensitisation comparable to low-dose metformin, with significantly better gastrointestinal tolerability. Combined myo-inositol/D-chiro-inositol in a 40:1 ratio (reflecting physiological proportions) appears to optimise ovarian function and hormonal outcomes. Some studies show improved oocyte quality and pregnancy rates in PMOS women undergoing IVF.

Complementary and Integrative Approaches

Many women with PMOS use complementary and integrative medicine (CIM) approaches alongside or instead of conventional treatments. Understanding the evidence — and the limitations of the evidence — for these approaches is important for person-centred care.

Evidence Summary for Complementary Approaches in PMOS

Vitamin D supplementation: Vitamin D deficiency is highly prevalent in PMOS — affecting 60–85% of women in some studies. Vitamin D has insulin-sensitising properties, and observational studies show inverse correlations between vitamin D levels and insulin resistance, testosterone, and menstrual irregularity in PMOS. Supplementation trials show modest improvements in insulin sensitivity and menstrual regularity in deficient women. Testing 25-hydroxyvitamin D and correcting deficiency is a reasonable, low-risk intervention.

Omega-3 fatty acids: Fish oil supplementation (2–4g/day EPA+DHA) reduces triglycerides and CRP in PMOS in multiple randomised trials. Anti-inflammatory and lipid-lowering effects are well-established. Omega-3 supplementation is particularly valuable for women with PMOS who have elevated triglycerides or cannot or will not take statin therapy.

Spearmint tea: Spearmint has mild anti-androgenic properties — attributed to its phytochemical content including rosmarinic acid and flavonoids. Two small randomised trials found that drinking 2 cups of spearmint tea daily reduced free testosterone levels and improved hirsutism scores over 30 days, compared to chamomile tea control. The effect size is modest, and spearmint tea should be considered a complementary rather than primary treatment for hyperandrogenism, but it is safe and accessible.

Acupuncture: Acupuncture for PMOS has been studied extensively, particularly in Scandinavian and Chinese research programmes. The evidence is mixed: some trials show improvements in ovulation frequency, menstrual regularity, and androgen levels, while others show no significant effect versus sham acupuncture. The 2026 consensus notes insufficient evidence to recommend acupuncture as a primary treatment but acknowledges its potential role as an adjunct, particularly for stress reduction and quality of life.

Cinnamon: Cinnamon (particularly Ceylon cinnamon) has shown insulin-sensitising effects in type 2 diabetes trials, and small PMOS studies suggest it may improve menstrual regularity and insulin sensitivity. Evidence is preliminary — controlled trials specifically in PMOS are limited — but the risk profile is favourable and cinnamon is a reasonable dietary addition.

PMOS Across the Life Span

Adolescence: Diagnosis and Early Intervention

PMOS frequently manifests in adolescence — often within 2–3 years of menarche — presenting with irregular cycles, acne, and weight gain. Early intervention is valuable but must be balanced with awareness that some degree of cycle irregularity is normal in the first post-menarchal years.

Studies show that adolescents with PMOS who receive early lifestyle and metabolic intervention achieve better long-term outcomes than those in whom hormonal birth control is used as the primary management without addressing metabolic drivers. The window of adolescence and early adulthood may represent a particularly impactful opportunity to interrupt the PMOS disease trajectory before metabolic damage accumulates.

Reproductive Years: Fertility and Pregnancy

The reproductive years bring the dual challenge of managing PMOS symptoms while preserving and optimising fertility. Pregnancy in PMOS carries elevated risks that require specific monitoring protocols.

Pregnancy Complications in PMOS

Women with PMOS have elevated risks of gestational diabetes (2.9 fold increased risk), gestational hypertension and pre-eclampsia (3.7 fold), preterm birth, and neonatal complications including admission to neonatal intensive care. These risks are substantially reduced by pre-conception optimisation of metabolic health. Metformin is often continued through the first trimester in women with PMOS to reduce miscarriage risk; evidence for continuation beyond the first trimester is mixed.

Perimenopause and Beyond

As oestrogen levels decline in perimenopause, the androgen-to-oestrogen ratio in PMOS often worsens before eventually improving. Interestingly, some women with PMOS notice cycle regularisation in their late 30s to early 40s as ovarian reserve declines — but metabolic risks including diabetes and cardiovascular disease continue to accrue.

Women with PMOS have higher rates of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidaemia in the menopausal transition compared to unaffected women of the same age and BMI. Long-term cohort studies suggest that women with PMOS who were not treated metabolically in their reproductive years carry a substantially higher cardiometabolic burden into older age.

Postmenopause: Long-Term Follow-Up

A common misconception is that PMOS is a condition of reproductive years that resolves after menopause. While some features — notably ovulatory dysfunction and the most pronounced hormonal cycling — do change in the postmenopausal period, the metabolic legacy of PMOS persists and in many women worsens.

PMOS in the Postmenopausal Years

Postmenopausal women with a history of PMOS show significantly higher rates of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and MASLD compared to matched postmenopausal women without PMOS history. The cardiometabolic damage accumulated over decades of insulin resistance and hyperandrogenism does not disappear with the cessation of ovarian function — it compounds. This means that women with PMOS require lifelong metabolic monitoring and cardiovascular risk management, not just during their reproductive years.

In the context of hormone therapy for menopausal symptoms, women with PMOS may benefit from specific formulations. Non-oral oestrogen delivery (transdermal or vaginal) avoids the hepatic first-pass effect that increases triglycerides and clotting factors with oral oestrogens — particularly relevant for women with PMOS who already carry elevated cardiovascular and thrombotic risk. Progestogen selection for endometrial protection should favour micronised progesterone over synthetic progestins where possible, given its more favourable metabolic and cardiovascular profile.

Genetics and the Biology of PMOS

A Polygenic Condition

PMOS does not follow a simple Mendelian inheritance pattern. Meta-analyses of large-scale genome-wide association studies confirm that PMOS has polygenic origins across neuroendocrine, metabolic, and reproductive pathways. Heritability estimates range from 50–80%, suggesting strong but complex genetic contributions.

Key Genetic Loci Implicated in PMOS

Genome-wide association studies have identified risk loci near genes involved in gonadotropin signalling (LHCGR, FSHR), insulin metabolism (INSR, IRS1), androgen biosynthesis (CYP11A1, CYP19A1), and adipogenesis (THADA, DENND1A). The DENND1A gene, expressed in theca cells, has been particularly replicated across multiple populations and may represent a core driver of ovarian androgen excess. Variants in the FSHR gene affecting FSH receptor sensitivity may explain the elevated AMH and arrested follicle phenotype.

Epigenetics and PMOS: Beyond the DNA Sequence

The genetic architecture of PMOS explains only part of its heritability. Emerging evidence points to epigenetic mechanisms — heritable changes in gene expression that occur without alterations to the underlying DNA sequence — as important contributors to PMOS risk and transmission.

Epigenetic Mechanisms in PMOS

DNA methylation: Studies of peripheral blood cells and ovarian tissue from women with PMOS show altered DNA methylation patterns at genes involved in androgen biosynthesis, insulin signalling, and inflammatory pathways. These methylation differences can be detected in the offspring of women with PMOS, raising the possibility of transgenerational epigenetic transmission — a mechanism by which the metabolic-hormonal environment of a mother's pregnancy might predispose her daughter to PMOS.

In utero androgen exposure: Animal models have demonstrated that exposing female foetuses to excess androgens during a critical developmental window programs them to develop PMOS-like phenotypes in adulthood — with elevated LH, insulin resistance, hyperandrogenism, and anovulation. This "prenatal androgenisation" hypothesis suggests that daughters of women with PMOS may be exposed to elevated intrauterine androgen levels, epigenetically programming their hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis before birth.

Histone modifications: Changes in histone acetylation and methylation in ovarian granulosa and theca cells affect the expression of steroidogenic enzymes in PMOS. These modifications may be driven by the insulin resistance and inflammatory environment of PMOS, creating a cellular memory that perpetuates androgen overproduction even when the hormonal environment improves temporarily.

A landmark 2025 study published in Nature Medicine compared the epigenomes of 400 women with PMOS and 400 matched controls across multiple tissues (blood, adipose, and endometrial). The study identified 37 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) that distinguished women with PMOS, including sites in LHCGR, AMH, and IGF1R promoter regions. Importantly, 12 of these DMRs were also present in cord blood samples of daughters born to women with PMOS — providing the first direct human evidence for transgenerational epigenetic transmission of PMOS risk.

The Male Phenotype Question

One of the unresolved debates in PMOS biology concerns male relatives of affected women. Male first-degree relatives of women with PMOS show elevated rates of insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and premature balding — suggesting that shared genetic variants predispose to a related but differently expressed condition in males.

One reason some workshop participants favoured "polyendocrine metabolic ovulatory syndrome" over "ovarian syndrome" was to allow for the possibility of a male phenotype of PMOS. Since males lack ovaries, "ovarian" in the name forecloses this conceptual possibility. The 2026 consensus acknowledges this as an open question: defining and characterising the male phenotype to assess male relatives of women with PMOS is listed as a priority research area.

In Vitro Disease Models: The Research Frontier

As of 2024–2026, researchers have successfully developed in vitro models of PMOS using human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology. These models allow for the first time the direct study of PMOS cellular biology without relying on animal models or invasive patient procedures.

iPSCs derived from individuals with PMOS can be reprogrammed into a pluripotent state and then specified to replicate PMOS-like cellular traits. Three-dimensional "organoid" models of ovarian and uterine tissue produced from iPSCs now provide a platform for testing new drug candidates and understanding the earliest cellular events in PMOS development. These models represent the most promising avenue for drug discovery in the condition since the discovery of metformin's utility.

PMOS and the Healthcare System: What Needs to Change

Medical Education: Training the Next Generation of Clinicians

One of the most durable legacies of the PCOS era will be the generation of clinicians trained in its gynaecological framework — who now need to update their practice to reflect the PMOS reconceptualisation. The 2026 consensus explicitly prioritises medical education reform as a key implementation target.

Medical Education Reform for PMOS

The 2026 implementation plan calls for PMOS to be incorporated into undergraduate and postgraduate curricula across internal medicine, endocrinology, obstetrics and gynaecology, general practice, dermatology, dietetics, and psychology — reflecting the multidisciplinary nature of the condition. Teaching should emphasise insulin resistance as the central metabolic driver, the importance of metabolic-first treatment sequencing, and the requirement for mental health screening at every assessment. Continuing professional development (CPD) modules for currently practising clinicians are planned through major international endocrinology and general practice societies in the 3-year rollout period.

Beyond the renaming itself, the education reform must address clinical bias — including weight bias, gender bias, and the historical tendency to normalise women's symptoms. Studies consistently show that women with PMOS experience higher rates of clinical dismissal, symptom minimisation, and weight-focused counselling without comprehensive metabolic evaluation. Structural anti-bias training is as important as clinical content reform.

Patient Advocacy and the Role of Community

The renaming of PCOS to PMOS did not happen in an academic vacuum. It was partly driven by patient advocacy — by the documented experiences of millions of women who found the old name confusing, stigmatising, and inadequate for describing what they were living with.

Patient Voices in the Renaming Process

Patient advocacy organisations — including PCOS Australia, Verity (UK), PCOS Challenge (US), and dozens of regional bodies — were formal co-participants in the global consensus process, not simply consulted afterthought voices. Patient experience data from the surveys explicitly shaped the name selection: respondents consistently ranked "accuracy," "reduced stigma," and "metabolic recognition" as the most important attributes of any new name. Survey participants specifically flagged that the word "cysts" created unnecessary fear, that the gynaecological framing had led them to be inadequately assessed for metabolic health, and that a name reflecting the hormonal-metabolic complexity of the condition would help them advocate for more comprehensive care.

Online patient communities — PCOS forums, Reddit communities, Instagram accounts with millions of followers — have played a significant role in the lived-experience conversation. The spread of accessible medical information through social media has been a double-edged sword: it has empowered women to self-advocate and seek better care, while also creating spaces for misinformation (particularly around unproven "cures," supplement marketing, and extreme dietary claims). With the PMOS rename, there is an opportunity to seed high-quality, accessible patient information through these channels — and patient organisations are already working on plain-language PMOS explainers, social media toolkits, and multilingual resources.

ICD and Billing Code Transition

Among the most practically complex challenges of the PCOS-to-PMOS rename is the update of international disease classification and billing systems. The ICD-11 code E28.2 currently captures PCOS; the 2026 implementation plan proposes introducing a new PMOS code with a cross-reference to E28.2, and a planned ICD-11 update scheduled for the 2027 WHO revision cycle.

Why Billing Codes Matter for PMOS

Disease classification codes do more than just organise medical records — they drive insurance reimbursement decisions, research funding allocations, pharmaceutical approval pathways, and population health statistics. The PCOS code historically classified the condition within ovarian disorders, limiting insurance approval for metabolic treatments (including metformin in some jurisdictions, and GLP-1 agonists in most). The introduction of a PMOS code — particularly if cross-classified under both endocrine disorders and metabolic diseases in addition to reproductive medicine — could substantially expand insurance coverage for the broader range of treatments validated by the new framework. Healthcare advocacy organisations are already preparing submissions to national insurance authorities in Australia, the United Kingdom, the United States, Canada, and the European Union arguing for expanded PMOS treatment coverage under the new classification.

The Global Consensus Process: How PMOS Was Born

Over a Decade of Advocacy

The renaming of PCOS to PMOS was not a sudden decision — it was the culmination of more than a decade of research, surveys, workshops, and international collaboration. The process was described in The Lancet as "unprecedented in scope and rigour."

Timeline of the Renaming Process

Pre-2015: Growing clinical and patient recognition that "PCOS" was inaccurate and harmful; informal calls for renaming in the literature.

2015–2020: Formal academic discussions begin; the Androgen Excess and PCOS Society and the Australian Centre for Research Excellence in Women's Health in Reproductive Life take leadership roles.

2020–2023: Global survey programme launches; over 22,000 people including patients, clinicians, and researchers from all world regions respond over multiple survey rounds.

2023–2025: Workshop series with 56 organisations; iterative refinement of candidate names; structured voting processes; patient advocacy groups co-design implementation strategies.

May 12, 2026: The Lancet publishes the consensus paper by Teede et al.; PMOS officially adopted; presented at the European Congress of Endocrinology, Prague; 8-stage implementation plan launched.

The Australian National Health and Medical Research Council funded the consensus centre. Governance involved 56 leading academic, clinical, and patient organisations spanning all world regions. The highest-ranked name in the global survey (Survey B) was "polyendocrine metabolic ovulatory syndrome" — but clinical workshop refinement changed "ovulatory" to "ovarian" to retain emphasis on ovarian biology. All but two workshop participants supported the final choice. The implementation rollout targets updates to guidelines in 195 countries over 3 years.

Implementation Challenges

Transitioning from a deeply entrenched term like "PCOS" — familiar to millions of patients, embedded in medical textbooks, coded into electronic health records, and encoded in insurance billing systems worldwide — is not trivial. The 2026 paper outlines 8 implementation stages, acknowledging the practical challenges of terminology transition at global scale.

The two workshop participants who opposed the renaming did so on grounds of "evolving genetic science" (the condition's biology is still being discovered, and any name risks becoming outdated) and "practical transition concerns" (updating ICD codes, insurance systems, medical curricula, patient materials, and electronic records across 195 countries is an enormous logistical undertaking). The consensus authors acknowledge these concerns but maintain that the scientific and patient welfare benefits of the new name outweigh the transition costs.

The 8-Stage PMOS Implementation Plan

The 2026 consensus outlines a structured 8-stage global implementation plan designed to be completed over a 3-year period. This level of implementation planning is unprecedented in medical renaming history and reflects the scale and complexity of the transition.

Implementation Stages

Stage 1 — Consensus publication and launch (Completed May 2026): Publication of the definitive consensus paper in The Lancet, simultaneous announcement at a major international congress, coordinated press release to international medical media, and patient organisation notification.

Stage 2 — International society adoption (2026–2027): Major endocrinology, reproductive medicine, and general practice societies in all 195 countries to formally adopt PMOS in their guidelines, position statements, and educational materials. Led by the Endocrine Society, European Society of Endocrinology, and the European Society for Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE).

Stage 3 — ICD code update (2027): WHO ICD-11 update to introduce PMOS diagnostic code with cross-reference to legacy PCOS code E28.2. National billing system updates to follow in subsequent 12 months.

Stage 4 — Medical education curriculum reform (2026–2028): Integration of PMOS framework into undergraduate and postgraduate curricula across all relevant specialties globally. CPD modules for practising clinicians.

Stage 5 — Patient resources (2026–2027): Development of plain-language PMOS patient information in all major world languages, including audio and video formats for low-literacy contexts. Multilingual social media toolkits for patient advocacy organisations.

Stage 6 — Clinical guideline updates (2026–2028): Evidence-based guideline updates in all jurisdictions to reflect metabolic-first treatment sequencing, expanded metabolic screening at diagnosis, and mental health integration.

Stage 7 — Research funding redirection (2026–2029): Advocacy to major national and international research funding bodies to expand PMOS funding beyond reproductive endocrinology to cardiology, metabolism, neuroscience, and epigenetics research streams.

Stage 8 — Implementation monitoring (ongoing from 2026): Structured global monitoring of implementation progress, including tracking of diagnostic rates, time-to-diagnosis, metabolic screening rates, and treatment pattern changes in representative health system datasets across at least 20 countries.

What This Means If You Have PCOS/PMOS

Your Diagnosis Didn't Change — The Understanding Did

If you have been diagnosed with PCOS, you still have the same condition. Nothing about your biology changed on May 12, 2026. What changed is the name, the scientific framing, and — most importantly for your care — the treatment priorities that flow from that framing.

Questions to Ask Your Doctor in Light of the PMOS Rename

1. Have my metabolic markers been checked? Ask for a fasting glucose, insulin (HOMA-IR), full lipid panel, and liver enzymes at baseline — not just hormonal markers.

2. What is my AMH level? AMH is increasingly important in PMOS diagnosis and monitoring of treatment response.

3. Is insulin resistance being treated, not just the symptoms? If you've only been offered the pill, ask about metformin, inositol supplementation, or other metabolic interventions.

4. Have GLP-1 medications been considered? If you have significant weight-driven PMOS or haven't responded to metformin, ask whether a GLP-1 agonist is appropriate for your case.

5. Is my mental health being screened proactively? Under the PMOS framework, anxiety and depression screening should be part of every initial assessment and follow-up visit.

6. Am I being monitored for endometrial cancer risk? If you are experiencing infrequent periods, ask about endometrial protection strategies.

7. Have my sleep quality and cardiovascular risk been assessed? Sleep apnoea screening and baseline cardiovascular risk markers should be part of comprehensive PMOS care.

8. Should my first-degree relatives be assessed? Daughters and sisters of women with PMOS have significantly elevated risk and may benefit from proactive assessment, particularly if they have irregular cycles, acne, or weight gain.

Living Well With PMOS: A Practical Framework

Managing PMOS effectively is not about achieving a perfect set of laboratory values — it is about reducing long-term risk, managing symptoms, preserving quality of life, and building a sustainable lifestyle that supports hormonal and metabolic health across the decades. The PMOS framework offers a more hopeful and actionable picture than the old PCOS model: by framing insulin resistance as the central driver, it gives women and their clinicians a clear, modifiable target.

Key Principles for Long-Term PMOS Management

Focus on process, not outcome: The most sustainable approach to PMOS management is building consistent habits — regular meals, regular movement, adequate sleep — rather than pursuing a specific weight target. The metabolic benefits of lifestyle changes in PMOS are measurable (lower HOMA-IR, lower testosterone, improved cycle regularity) regardless of whether significant weight loss occurs.

Work with your body's rhythms: Understanding your cycle — even if it is irregular — through basal body temperature tracking, LH testing, or hormonal cycle monitoring apps provides useful data for self-management and for fertility planning. Many women with PMOS do ovulate, just less frequently and less predictably than women without the condition.

Build your care team deliberately: The ideal PMOS care team includes a GP or endocrinologist who understands the metabolic dimensions of the condition, a dietitian with PMOS experience, and access to a psychologist or counsellor. Dermatologists, fertility specialists, and sleep physicians may be needed at different life stages. You are entitled to advocate for comprehensive care — and the PMOS rename, by formally establishing the multidisciplinary nature of the condition, strengthens that advocacy.

PMOS is a lifelong condition that can be effectively managed but not cured by any current intervention. Evidence-based approaches — lifestyle, metformin, inositols, OCPs, anti-androgens, GLP-1 agonists — are well characterised. Any intervention claiming to "cure" PMOS or "heal your ovaries" should be discussed with a knowledgeable clinician.
